Abstract

My paper explores the dynamics of twentieth-century literary manifestations of communist internationalism in the southern Indian region of Kerala vis-a-vis the Soviet Union. Drawing from Allan Pasco's notion of the literature-archive, I illustrate how communist internationalism in Kerala was expressed in terms of worldmaking in three chronological periods in response to political and literary shifts at the local, national and global levels. The first period deals with early engagements of the literature-archive from the 1910s till the 1930s, marked by a yearning for an amorphously defined new world characterised by individualised political canonical engagements with individuals associated with Marxism as well as situational identification with the worlds of Tsarist Russia and Soviet Union. The prominence of the progressive literature movement from the late-1930s until the late 1940s resulted in the emergence of multiple literary-political circles that wrought various imaginations of a socialist realist egalitarian internationalist world, producing and contesting various visions of Soviet Union. Finally, in the third period, I deal with how the Cold War context enabled the perpetuation of these literary contestations as well as the diffusion of a new mode of the cultural internationalism of the post-Stalinist Soviet Union from the 1950s till the 1980s making and contesting Soviet utopias in travelogs and children's literature.

In 1957, the southern Indian state of Kerala became the world's second democratic polity to elect a communist government.1 Five years later in 1962, K Damodaran, one of Kerala's foremost communist theoreticians travelled to the Soviet Union, for medical treatment. As he lay bedridden in a hospital, he read an issue of Pravda, the official newspaper of the CPSU (Communist Party of the Soviet Union) and was extremely disturbed by an article commemorating Napoleon's retreat after failing to annex Moscow. The deification of the Tsarist regime combined with the ‘diatribe’ against Napoleon dismayed Damodaran, prompting him to write a letter to Pravda's editor. He argued that Napoleon's incomplete attempts towards ‘a bourgeoisie-democratic revolution, even in a distorted … form’ was undoubtedly the lesser evil compared to Tsarist absolutism. To put it differently, ‘the nationalist fervour of the article was horrifying to me.’2
Based on decades-long extensive engagement with CPSU publications and Soviet literature, Damodaran had imbibed and passionately propagated a literary world-making project which had become highly influential (and at times arguably hegemonic in the 1940s and early 1950s) in Kerala. This was a particular mode of a communist internationalist ethos which saw the world as linear, headed towards progress translating to the inevitable radical revolution and equal future, symbolised by the Soviet Union. While the ‘triumph’ of nationalism over the past decade and parliamentary democratic compromises made by his own erstwhile radical communist party in his homeland may have muddied this vision to a degree, the nationalist endorsement of Tsarist feudalist absolutism, a highly outmoded stage, and a vestige of the past in this communist internationalist telos, in the very Fatherland would have been jarring, to say the least. Far from an ostensibly intellectual disagreement, this episode exemplified a clash of two world making projects mediated through literature, laying bare the contradictions between a post-colonial world of communist internationalism and the high noon of Stalinist and Cold War nationalism.
Many accounts of global history assume a transition from the high noon of internationalism in the early post First World War era to the demise of internationalism and the associated triumph of the nation state by the Second World war and decolonization.3 In the specific context of communist internationalism, most literature presumes a Leninist moment right after the establishment of the October revolution and the Comintern in 1919 which saw a dizzying variety of internationalisms which were gradually swallowed by the Stalinist nationalism of the Soviet Union (accompanied by the rise of fascism and Nazism). I complicate this linear narrative by highlighting the tensions of multiple modes of communist internationalism and nationalism often jostling against each other, within the same timeframe, as exemplified by the above literary encounter of Damodaran. While the Soviet worlds had long shifted to a nationalist worldmaking project dominated by the Soviet Union, the likes of Damodaran continued to hold on to whatever was left of an internationalist mode of global class struggles.
In this paper, I seek to trace the history of this literary communist internationalist project in Kerala, primarily vis-à-vis the space of Russia and Soviet Union. I first contextualise this work against the backdrop of existing debates on Kerala's cosmopolitanism, extending the notion of the regionality of Kerala to focus on its horizontal affiliations with Russia and the Soviet Union. I then draw from and extend Allan Pasco's work on a literature-archive as well as outline the contours of a discursive worldmaking logic in order to make sense of Kerala's history of communist internationalism. The first period deals with the early engagements of the literature-archive from the 1910s till the 1930s, marked by a yearning for an amorphously defined new world characterised by individualised political and canonical engagements with individuals associated with Marxism as well as a situational identification with the worlds of Tsarist Russia and Soviet Union. The next section deals with the prominence of the progressive literature movement from the late 1930s until the late 1940s resulting in the emergence of multiple literary-political ‘circles’ that wrought various imaginations of a socialist realist egalitarian internationalist world, producing and contesting various visions of Soviet Union. In the third section, I deal with how the Cold War context enabled the perpetuation of these literary contestations as well as the diffusion of a new mode of the cultural internationalism of the post-Stalinist Soviet Union from the 1950s to the 1980s, making and re-making Soviet worlds, especially in children's literature.

Kerala's communist internationalism: Worldmaking and the literary archive

The term Kerala is often used to signify the region straddling the South-Western coast of India. This current sub-nation state was constituted in 1957 by merging the princely states of Travancore and Cochin, as well as the Malabar region from the British province of Madras, often unified by the linguistic marker of Malayalam language. With the continued prominence of communism and limited penetration of Hindutva in electoral terms as well as an idiosyncratic Kerala model signifying high human development indicators juxtaposed with low growth in the 1970s–1990s, Kerala has always been a space of scholarly fascination.4Kerala has a vibrant history of social reform movements mostly from the late-nineteenth century onwards against some of the worst forms of caste discrimination which existed in South Asia, exacerbated by colonial capitalist modernity. This tradition of protests was continued by radical peasant and labour movements in the 1930s and 1940s, legitimised by communism. Initially forming a left faction within the dominant nationalist Congress party called the Congress Socialist Party, the communists would later break away from Congress to form the Communist party in Kerala in 1939. Building on the consolidation of a near hegemonic presence of communism in the 1940s and the early 1950s, the Communist party would come to power in Kerala's first elections in 1957. While several scholars have sought to explain the penetration and success of communism in Kerala during this period, I do not intend to add to these explanations.5 My focus, rather, is on two aspects which have been relatively neglected within this scholarship: the projects of communist internationalism and of worldmaking.
These aspects need to be viewed against a vibrant debate on Kerala and its transnational histories of cosmopolitanisms.6 Empirically, a number of works have highlighted the entanglements of Kerala with the larger world beyond the Indian nation-state spanning the Middle East, Southeast Asia and Europe.7 Against this backdrop, Devika brought attention to the notion of Kerala's regionality (rather than it being a region), emphasising the constructedness and the contingency of the region. In addition, moving beyond the narratives which emphasise Kerala's regional exceptionalism (the supposed Kerala model) or its vertical linkages vis-à-vis the nation, she argued for the need to explore horizontal linkages constituting the region of Kerala vis-à-vis other geographical entities and spaces. Various social groups in Kerala have related to the globe in different modes (such as casteist, subaltern or cosmopolitanism) depending on ‘internal social dynamics’ and their differential integration with the world capitalist system.8 Using the example of Menon's exploration of the locally rooted cosmopolitanism of amateur historian Kesari Balakrishna Pillai vis-à-vis Europe, she argues for the recovery of various cosmopolitanisms of Kerala which declare affiliations with other worlds and places.9 I extend these explorations, focussing on the horizontal internationalist affiliations of Kerala with the Soviet Union.
I argue that these affiliations produced multiple worldmaking projects, which can be uncovered by an analysis of the literary archive of Kerala. Prior to this, a few conceptual clarifications are necessary. As regards the notion of the literary archive, I draw from and extend Pasco's theorisation of literature as an archive.10 Pasco argued for the treatment of literature as an archive on multiple grounds. Besides the limited presence of official archives in certain periods, he argued that literature, exemplified in ‘novels, plays, poems, and essays, many of which include extensive social commentary’ provide a keen understanding of the fears, desires, fantasies, beliefs, values, and aspirations of the people during an era, adding depth and breadth to history.11 He however qualified this use, insisting on the repetition and recurrence on certain themes throughout literature.12
Often traced to the canonical formulation of Getachew to signify an anti-systemic vision which translates to ‘a rejection of unequal status built into the international system as a consequence of prior imperial projects’, the notion of worldmaking has informed a range of recent scholarship across multiple fields spanning historical sociology, geography, international relations, moral philosophy, anthropology and international law.13 In this regard, some scholars like Quah have recently sought to theoretically disaggregate the notion of worldmaking into different dimensions, such as discursive, institutional, and dramaturgical, each accompanied by their distinct logics.14 My work explores and extends this discursive logic of worldmaking which seeks to explore the ‘intellectual, linguistic, and political contexts’ of texts and speech-acts as well as a discussion of the linguistic choices including content, narration and tropes employed. In this regard, I draw from Martin and Sneegas, to conceive a critical worldmaking approach.15 Moving beyond the ‘intellectual, linguistic, and political contexts’ which informed the literary archive in Kerala across various time periods, this approach calls for a critical engagement with the imagined literary worlds as well as story worlds.16 It also further signifies an engagement with the production as well as struggles over these worlds. The literary archive of Kerala (largely in Malayalam language) was integral to the project of communist worldmaking and internationalism. Firstly, given that the official colonial state archives securitised the communists and portrayed them as ‘devoid of any complexity, except as dogged, irascible individuals implacably opposed to the Empire’ with the Communist Party banned till 1942, literature becomes critical, if not indispensable, as an alternate archive to understand the fantasies and the aspirations which revealed themselves in the worlds the communists envisaged, especially in the earlier decades.17 Secondly, archiving often became a highly conscious and deliberate anti-systemic world-making project in Kerala in a Getachewian sense where nationalism was inextricably tied to forging a new world free of domination through literary and artistic interventions.18 Thirdly, a critical worldmaking approach also recognises that myriad genres of literature in Kerala, from travelogues to short stories, served as ‘constitutive activities of representation’, making, de-making and re-making communist worlds and spaces.19 Lastly, literature also served to archive and canonise certain men, enabling a certain mode of making sense of the world and the international.
A major implication of my work lies in the assertion that these linkages themselves were responsible for producing and imagining a specific vision of the space of Kerala. Against the anxieties of the marginality of Kerala vis-à-vis other sub-national regions/states and the larger empire/nation state, communist practices of world-making were integral to the constitution of a narrative of exceptionalism which continues to be central to the imagination to Kerala as a distinct region. On the one hand, this materiality of communist exceptionalism was manifested in several interventions like land and labour reforms as well as a history of radical class struggles which were far more pronounced than most regions of India. On the other hand, this exceptionalism also imagined a communist landscape free from the regressive forces of fascism, imperialism, feudalism and capitalism amidst a widespread prevalence of an experience of solidarity with the likes of the regions of the Second World especially the Soviet Union. In fact, this imagination and materiality as an exceptional communist space was partly responsible for the widespread characterization of Kerala as India's problem state, which was particularly prevalent in nationalist and Western governmental and media circles, facilitated by Cold War exigencies.20
In the following sections, I highlight how various imaginations of communist internationalism and worldmaking cohered around the literature-archive in three chronological periods. In each of these sections, I first sketch out the corresponding archive and its broader global and local context which informed its production. Subsequently, using select passages from selected texts and literature, I illuminate the visions and projects of internationalism and worldmaking they embody.

Early affiliations: Situational identification and individual engagements

A number of movements emerged by the early two decades of the 20th century, challenging the systemic worldmaking projects of imperialism, manifested in struggles against the rule of the British-enabled princely states. There also emerged a number of reformist and radical anti-caste movements spearheaded by the likes of Sree Narayana Guru, Ayyankali and Sahodaran Ayyappan.21 It was against this local landscape of intense political churn that the Bolshevik revolution emerged as a major global symbol of hope. People across the world saw their national emancipation as inseparable from anti-imperial emancipation across the world. With an increasing recognition of the interconnectedness of the world, they saw themselves as citizens of the world, hoping to forge the world anew.22 Kerala was exemplaryin this regard.
Since the latter decades of the 19th century, Kerala had already experienced a ‘hardening of colonial modernity’ manifested in the increased number of middle classes as well as the emergence of a robust print media and culture.23 These developments led to a rich literary archive characterised by a wide range of early engagements with various facets of communism. One of the earliest mentions of socialism and communism appeared in a magazine called Vidyavinodini a mere seven years after the death of Marx.24 These interventions continued in the early decades of the twentieth century, with the first biography of Marx in any Indian language published by Swadeshabhimani Ramakrishna Pillai as early as 1912, half a decade before the Russian revolution. The revolution was also widely discussed in periodicals like Arayan, Mitavadiand Atmaposhini. The likes of Marx and Lenin frequently featured in the revived Swadeshabhimani throughout the 1920s. The first book on the Russian revolution was published in 1931 titled ‘Agniyum Sphulingavum’ by P Kesavadev. By the 1930s, a number of articles on the Russian revolution and post-revolution reconstruction were featured in leading outlets like Mathrubhumi and its weekly. The Russian revolution and the Soviet Union were also a constant theme which recurred during the speeches of the likes of Sahodaran Ayyappan, C Kesavan, K M Ibraham and P Kesavadev.25
The literary archive during this timeframe thus featured newspapers, magazines, books and speeches. An examination of this archive highlights certain interesting themes. Drawing from Jelnikar, one can see a thread of ‘internationalism of situational identification’ wherein the authors of these texts often exhibited a broad individual and societal situational identification with the oppressed and suffering people across the world (especially in the Russian empire) as well as a millenarian and romantic faith in an inevitable new dawn or future.26 This was also linked to a diverse range of individual engagements with individuals associated with Marxism, informed by a wide range of political and ideological positions and often devoid of a clear theoretical sophistication.27 Such engagements introduced new worlds of various hues to the Malayalee.
Many works on the Russian empire and the October revolution exhibited a situational identification under structurally similar forms of oppression and subjugation. Drawing from critics like Leelavathy, the ‘heart wrenching tales of the oppressed’ underlined by the ‘philosophy of equality’ was highly resonant to Kerala's public who were suffering from analogous conditions of feudalistic caste-based structures.28
Malayali accounts of the Tsarist empire often featured highly polemical and melodramatic accounts of the lives of the oppressed in Tsarist Russia. Kesavadev in his ‘Agniyum Sphulingavum’ portrays pre-revolution Russia as a land of unparalleled exploitation, where every single being lived for the Tsar and human beings lived like animals.29This situational affinity did not merely translate to a relatability with the suffering but also entailed a millenarian faith in a new dawn symbolised by the revolution which could be replicated in Kerala as well. In one of his speeches during the 1932 Abstention movement, aimed at responsible government in Travancore kingdom, Kesavadev drew parallels between the fall of the royal family and the inevitable demise of the Travancore kingdom. Sahodaran Ayyappan's poem, for instance, highlight this oppression and millenarianism perfectly:
'Perennial servility was it for long, The land of Russia had it to mourn, But with glory at the end did it attain, A model freedom of world renown.
Create here, oh! Comrades (Sakhaakkale), and strive apace, And dazzle the world with echoing histories.'30
The politics of similitude can also be seen in narrations of developments in post-revolution Soviet Union. For instance, a Mathrubhumi Aazchapathippu report on Soviet agricultural practices by Pulimath Raghavan Nair concluded with the assertion that Soviet rule has ‘immensely contributed to the hopes, happiness, aspirations and comfort of Russian peasants. When will such a fate befall the depressed and hungry peasants of India?’31
The literary internationalism that emerged was characterised by a wide range of individual idiosyncrasies which accompanied the engagements with the Russian revolution and Marxism during this period. Marxism thus was a realisation of individual fantasies, often shorn of a sophisticated theoretical rigour. The Russian revolution and Marxism often seamlessly fitted into a wide range of political projects including nationalist, anti-monarchical as well as anti-caste struggles, which were quite interlinked. For instance, Ramakrishna Pillai recast Marx's biography in terms of his own autobiographical concerns; for him, Marxism signified the end of the rule of the king reflecting his opposition to the native princely state of Travancore.32 In a speech during the Vaikom Satyagrahaa of 1924–25, Sahodaran Ayyappan invoked the Russian Revolution as an exemplar for the Ezhavas to strive for the end of monarchical rule in Travancore.33
Most of the literary engagements during this timeframe were characterised by particular conception of the world dominated by a heroic model of biography, reflective of similar trends elsewhere. Characterised by a ‘cradle-to grave narration the life of a distinct, distinctive … a distinguished individual’ in scrupulous detail, it reflected the Carlyelian notion of an ‘individual life as expression and creator of an age.’34 Kesavadev introduces the life of Trotsky in a similar fashion arguing, ‘the task of a historian is to document the achievements of an individual for humanity.’35
This entrenched a particular form of world which progressed and transformed through the acts of great men. They thus constitute ‘an improving example for others to follow.’ Ramakrishna Pillai alludes to the lessons we can learn from the lives of the “greatest among mortals” and the need for introducing them to the readers in Kerala. Many works during this period sought to integrate Marx as well as the individuals associated with the Russian Revolution into this canon of the ‘benefactors of humanity.’36 There were rigorous discussions in Swadeshabhimani, for instance, on what constituted greatness with a number of accounts giving primacy to selflessness, sacrifice and leadership. According to Sriman K Kumaran, Lenin ‘carried the weight of the crumbling Russian empire on his shoulders to create a great world without Deceit or selfishness and discipline.’37 For Kalyani Amma, Marx ‘sacrificed his life and blood to establish dharma as a pursuit of life to work for a love, truth and brotherhood.’38 Marxists individuals found themselves among the company of a bewildering range of ‘great men’ including Gandhi, William Wilberforce, Oliver Cromwell, Napolean, Jesus and Mohammed. Drawing from Rial, this ‘deference in life writing did not displace the … gender hierarchies.’39 For instance, an expansive section of Ramakrishna Pillai's biography, more substantial than even his philosophy, celebrates the reproductive labour as a good mother and a dutiful wife undertaken by his wife, Jenny.
It is also important to mention Kerala's engagements with Russian literature during the period, especially through the vast oeuvre of amateur historian Kesari Balakrishna Pillai who accorded a high degree of prominence towards Russian literature and authors like Mayakovsky and Chekhov. However, as opposed to a sole deference for the great men and literature of Soviet Union, his interventions should be seen as part of his larger deliberate worldmaking project. Pillai founded the tabloid weekly Kesari in April 1930 as well as published a number of essays in periodicals including inter alia Aazchapathippu, Samadarsi and Athmaposhini. Through these interventions, Pillai expanded the horizons of the literary imagination of Malayalees, introducing a wide range of world literature spanning essays and translations across Europe and Asia.40 He thus sought to archive a cosmopolitan canon of world literature which was linked to the new world that he envisaged. In other words, this was a highly deliberate and conscious political act as he believed that art and literature were indispensable to the imagination of a new world that was coming into existence.41 This new world was committed to a scientific modern sensibility, a radical egalitarian ethic and the freedom of the human spirit, which could not be bound by confines of narrow projects including the dominant Gandhian nationalism in India, which he emphatically rejected. In its foreword to his collection of essays ‘Navalokam’ (New World), he enjoins about the people of Kerala to aid the Western world in this actualization through art and literature, introducing Malayalees to a canon that would ‘arouse Malayalam literature from its classical stupor to the recognition of everyday realities and injustices.’42 In building a highly cosmopolitan canon, he also brought a wide range of literary currents in the West in dialogue with the writers of Kerala.43 He also introduced a wide range of Western literary currents spanning multiple genres in his work, Roopamanjari.44
As early as 1929, Pillai championed the cause of establishing a living and progressive literature in Kerala.45 This would come to fruition nearly a decade later in the form of a Lively Literature Movement in 1937 which led to the production of a substantial rich literary archive spanning multiple genres. This produced novel modes of communist worldmaking, which constitute the next section.

Progressive literature and social realism from the 1930s to the 1940s

The emergence of progressive literature in Kerala was primarily spearheaded by the Lively Literature Movement manifested in the form of the formation of the Jeevat Sahitya Sanghom (Association of the Literature of Life) in 1937. The prominence of the progressive literature movement in the late 1930s resulted in the emergence of multiple literary-political circles that wrought various imaginations of a socialist realist egalitarian internationalist world, producing and contesting various visions of the Soviet Union.
The emergence of this movement has to be understood against the backdrop of the CPSU-led Comintern's calls for a global intellectual front as well as the emergence of socialist Realism as the hegemonic literary expression in the Soviet Union. Firstly, the forging of a broad anti-imperialistic democratic front of intellectuals across the world had always been a key priority of the Comintern ever since its outset in 1919, with the October revolution. This influenced several interventions in the 1920s and early 1930s ranging from the International Workers Relief (1922), World Congress against colonial oppression and imperialism (1927), War Resisters International (1931) as well as meetings of the International Congress of Writers for the Defence of Culture (1935). These saw the active participation of a large number of intellectuals ranging from Gorky, Einstein and Russell to Mulk Raj Anand and Rabindra Nath Tagore at various points of time.46 As far as Kerala was concerned, the watershed moment was the declaration of the Seventh Congress of the Comintern in 1935 to create a united anti-imperial and anti-fascist front which propelled the formation of the Indian Progressive Writers' Association at the national level in India, followed by the Living Literature Movement in Kerala in 1937.47
Meanwhile there were several shifts in the political landscape of Kerala in the 1930s. While the mainstream Congress had emerged hegemonic in the twenties in Kerala, it was suffering from the contradictions between the conservative orientation of its bourgeoisie-feudal leadership and the radical politics of impatience among the masses exacerbated by caste-based and class-based injustices and oppression. For several dissatisfied Congress activists like EMS Namboodiripad and AK Gopalan, who would become prominent communists later, their incarceration in Kannur jail following the Gandhian civil disobedience movements proved a major turning point.48 These jails proved to ‘be universities for communists’, highlighting how colonial policies to suppress anti-colonial political activity unintentionally fostered new networks of resistance and dissent.49 As AK Gopalan reminiscence later, their exposure to communist ideas mediated through radical and communist fellow prisoners from the northern parts of India enabled them to think from an internationalist perspective. For him, the global was inseparable from the immediate concerns of the local. Following their release, they spent the next decade organizing peasants and labourers in unions and leading radical struggles against landlords, the imperial state and the native princely states, inculcating a spirit of defiance.50 Against the backdrop of the ban of the Communist party of India by the empire, they primarily operated through a left faction of the Congress – the Communist Socialist Party (CSP).
The Living Literature movement was primarily spearhead by a group of CSP leaders like E M S. and K Damodaran. While the LLM was primarily helmed by party activists and intellectuals, this shifted with the Shoranur conference of 1944 where the movement was rechristened as the Progressive Literature Movement (PLM).51 The Shoranur conference saw an active degree of participation from writers in Kerala, securing a high degree of legitimacy within literary circles. The PLM hosted three more conferences with the last two conferences marred by differences between party activists and litterateurs, eventually paving way for its split in 1949. Despite this split, the living literature and the progressive literature movements had a seismic literary impact on Malayalam literature, resulting in the production of a number of texts spanning a wide range of genres including short stories, novels, poems, literary criticism and plays.52
With this increasing prominence of living and progressive literature, socialist realism emerged as the dominant organizing framework of Malayalam literature. As mentioned before, ‘Socialist realism’ had been declared as the mandatory official method of literature for all writers in Soviet Union. In the Soviet Union, it was characterized by key principles including ‘ideological commitment (ideinost), party-mindedness (partiinost), popular spirit (narodnost), historicism, and typicality.53However, it would be fallacious to indicate an original/copy model whereby socialist realist ideas travelled from the Soviet core to the periphery of the progressive literature movement in Kerala.54 These principles were unevenly adopted against the backdrop of the specific local socio-political contexts of Kerala.
The socialist realist shift culminated in a different mode of communist worldmaking. Far from an amorphous millenarian conception of a new dawn or era in the earlier decades, the literary circles and party politician circles within the movement emphatically declared that art is not for art's sake and ought to be political. The Marxist literary critic Joseph Mundassery argued that authors have the responsibility to ruthlessly oppose all the institutions, policies and people that stand in the way of progress.55 Mundassery also extended Kesari's worldbuilding quest for a new literature for a new age, introducing literary currents from the West to the readers of Kerala.56 His works like Prayanam explored the literary history in a teleological Marxist fashion, highlighting the close interconnections between feudalism and classicism as well as capitalism and romanticism, giving way to socialism and realism. Given his faith in the telos of the socialist world, literature ought to have a progressive literary agenda for the emancipation of mankind. Building a Marxist cosmopolitan canon, he sought to bring medieval and modern Malayalam literature in conversation with the debates of Marxist literary analysis in the West.57
During this time, Marxist intellectuals like Damodaran translated the official history of the CPSU. The 1930s and 40s also saw an efflorescence of translations of the works of Lenin and Marx, spearheaded by the likes of Damodaran, EMS, Unniraja and M S Devadas, among others. Based on these translations, the likes of Damodaran highlighted a much more theoretically sophisticated understanding of communist internationalism moving beyond individualised negotiations with the Marxist oeuvre, and situational identification with the oppressed.58 His works consistently imagined a deterministic world headed towards the inexorable and inevitable socialist future, unfolding accorded to the Marxist laws of historicism in accordance with the dialectics of class struggle.59Against the backdrop of his deterministic view of the world, he argued that the role of an author is to study these laws of the society and reflect them in this work. The creator ought to transcend his class interests and glorify the progressive forces as well as reify the regressive forces.60
This was often attained in practice through a melodramatic mode of narration. See for instance, Damodaran's Pattabaakki (Rent arrears), considered by some as the first example of socialist realism. This play highlighted the travails of the family of Kittuni burdened by indebtness and constant exploitation by his landlord and associates. This forces Kittunni to steal some grains of rice resulting in an imprisonment. During this period, his mother dies of hunger, and he finally returns to see his sister taking to prostitution to support the family.61 Throughout this play, Damodaran employs a melodramatic scheme where good and evil are active forces, staging a hyperbolic and heightened drama between them. As Peter Brooks argues, this melodramatic narration ‘locates, makes evident, legible and operates the large choices of being that we deem of overwhelming importance.’62 The landlord-state nexus is portrayed as exaggeratingly diabolical in terms of financial, physical and sexual exploitation. The larger choice that he values of importance becomes a socialist society as exemplified by the words of Kittuni's friend Mohammed, ‘the capitalist has no heart or kindness, a man can only live as a human in socialist society.’ Kesavadev's ‘Odayil Ninnu’ is another socialist realist classic which deploys melodrama quite skilfully. The protagonist is a rickshawpuller Pappu, who is the perfect epitome of a socialist man. He does not take orders and devotes his life to protest, exhibiting constant defiance against evil landlords and capitalists. Central to the narrative of ‘Odayil Ninnu’ is the melodramatic trope of a person who progressively sacrificed himself to tuberculosis, engaged in a life of masculine defiant labour for the family he adopts.63
This socialist world inhabited by defiantly masculine socialist men sat within the context of prevalent norms of gender. This was hardly surprising given the homosociality of libraries and reading rooms as well as the very friendships which constituted ideas.64 This socialist realism operated against the backdrop of a mode of middle-class Malayali sexual morality which had emerged hegemonic in the fifties, promoting ‘self-discipline as the major mode of regulating desire, heterosexuality as the major mode of defining sexual desire and practice, patriarchy as the normative mode of organizing relations between the sexes, and the ideal of the modern nuclear family.’65 An essay of M S Devadas for instance illustrated the paranoia of the Malayali socialist man with unconventional modes of sexuality and sexual interactions expressed in some of the progressive literature.66 As Arunima points out, the Communist party never articulated a radical critique of marriage and family.67 The socialist realist world imagined by Marxist intelligentsia including EMS and Damodaran also prevented a substantial engagement with caste as a category. As Menon explains, this was partly due to the Brahminical appropriation of the historical materialist view of the world resulting in an overarching notion of progress and evolutionism where the likes of caste and other regressive customs were moribund vestiges of a bygone past which would be transcended inevitably in an inexorable quest towards the future world of progress. This assertion of ‘the pastness of caste … denied their tangibility in the present and their continuing legacy’ prevented a sustained negotiation with caste-in-itself.68
In actively building this socialist world inhabited by socialist men, the writers and party activists often looked to the Soviet Union as a beacon of hope. As Damodaran argued, ‘The Soviet Union provides an indication of where our country should be tomorrow. Stalin built a world which gave hope to crores of common people and revolutionaries.’ He reminisced later, ‘We identified ourselves with the Soviet Union completely.’69 In the Malayalam socialist realist ontology, Kerala was in the present, where the ‘visible features of communism’ were revealed at every turn headed inexorably towards the joyous ‘future-present’ of the Soviet future, symptomatic of the ‘revolutionary winds blowing across the world.’70 Thus for the likes of party workers like Krishna Pillai and Damodaran, the Soviet Union was characterised hyperbolically in terms of a socialist utopia, characterised by a perpetual end of class contradictions with countless peasants owning land.71
However, the socialist realist worldmaking project was rife with severe tensions and was heavily contested both from within the movement and by outsiders. As mentioned before, a major theme that was highlighted within the movement was the notion of the Stalin-led Soviet Union as the consolidated utopia which emerged as a telos for the socialist realist worldmaking projects. However, this came under increasing challenges from a wide range of journalists and authors outside the movement (e.g. satirist Sanjayan) who foregrounded the ‘satanist’ purges carried out by Stalin in the 1930s, condemning the violent character of the Soviet regime.72
The internal tensions within the Progressive Literature movement also progressively came to the fore. While these contestations encompassed a wide range of disagreements, a comprehensive discussion of these disagreements, falls beyond the scope of this paper. Instead, I focus specifically on the contestations related to the worldmaking project. As mentioned earlier, initially the movement was largely led by active party workers. While it was accorded greater legitimacy within the literary community in 1944, there still were differences between the circles of mainstream communists and those of the literary community. As Mundassery recalled, the latter camp included the likes of Kesavadev, M P Paul, Thakazhi and Kuttipuzha, who had broad socialist leanings but were not Marxist themselves.73 The communists progressively insisted on the Stalinist partinost position arguing for a mandatory party membership for authors. An essay (later submitted as a manifesto to the PLM in 1948) jointly written by EMS, Damodaran, C Unniraja, KK Variyar and MS Devadas highlighted party membership as integral to the notion of ‘progress.’ As Achutha Menon (who later became the communist Chief Minister) himself acknowledged that the essay was framed in a way that implied anyone who was not a member of the Communist Party would be considered an adversary to a progressive world. They enjoined their literary counterparts to engage in armed class struggles using the examples of British Marxists like Ralph Fox who was killed in the Spanish Civil War. This new criterion of progress led to a number of verbal offensives against the likes of Changambazha, Kesavadev and Kesari (who was labelled a bourgeoisie bookworm by EMS). The likes of Chandrasekharan argue that this shift to a Stalinist partinost position (party-mindedness) was necessitated by the changed context of Indian independence and the Calcutta thesis of the Communist party which called for arms struggles. While a common opposition to imperialism had cohered around a worldbuilding based on a broad notion of progress earlier within the movement, the post-imperial context of a party preparing for armed revolution required a far more active participation from the masses, including intellectuals.74
This Stalinist position was contested by the members of literary circles using discourses shaped by their respective engagements with communism and Russia. In a sense, this period saw the conflicts and tensions between the individualistic mode of affiliations with the Russia (which had germinated in the previous period) and doctrinaire party-based worldmaking projects. Multiple Russian literary worlds collided in these arguments. In a speech at the Thrissur PLM conference of 1948, the literary critic M P Paul endorsed a Leninist world of independence for the writer as opposed to a Stalinist world of censorship. He further advocated an aesthetics of Trotskyite literature as opposed to a ‘lifeless’ Stalinist literature. He also questioned the deterministic dialectical worlds of the party circles ‘Class wars have replaced Om as the dogmatic mantra today.’ His insistence on referring to the Soviet Union as the ‘Russian empire’ was itself a highly political act of highlighting the continuing imperialism of a supposedly anti-imperial space. The disagreements between both these factions of the PLM finally led to the split of the movement in 1949 when a party-influenced motion which sought to extend the membership to non-authors was rejected.75
By then, the Cold War had commenced and would enable the perpetuation of these contestations in a different manner. The Cold war would also enable novel modes of worldmaking, which constitute the focus of the next chapter.

Cold war internationalism: Continuing contestations and children's literature

'There is a land called the Soviet UnionWhat a fortune it would be to visit this place'K P Gopalakrishnan76
Throughout the Cold War period, the Soviet Union hosted a number of delegations from the 1950s to the 1980s who documented their experiences through travelogues. Drawing from Jayathilaka, these travelogues were important world-making endeavours, creating, de-creating and re-creating ‘people, places and pasts’ of the Soviet Union.77Kerala also saw a number of travelogues during this period which made and re-made the worlds of the Soviet Union. See for instance, AK Gopalan's travelog narrating his visit of the Soviet Union during 1952–53 for the 19th CPSU party congress. Narrated in extremely affectively charged terms, it veers from awe and exuberance for the Soviet ‘utopia’ to crushing melancholy and pathos towards the death of Stalin. He entrenched and re-made the Russian worlds, evidencing Damodaran's hyperbolic narrations on Stalin and the Soviet Union. Watching a Russian ballet, for instance, he concluded, ‘No wonder the Soviet Union remains first in the world in arts.’78 He also extensively described the high modernist socialist utopia of large-scale dams, agricultural projects and industries where ‘all workers work with a smile.’ Viewing the processions in the wake of the death of Stalin, he concluded that he ‘was a father to each and every one in the Soviet Union.’79 Another travelogue written by the P Balagangadhara Menon, the president of the Indo-Soviet Cultural Council, as part of a lawyers' delegation, re-made the utopia highlighting the absence of beggars and the freedom to propagate religion in the Soviet Union.80 Nayanar, for instance, invoked Tagore to indicate that his Soviet visit as part of a party delegation deemed his life complete.81
The narratives of these works often remained highly defensive and protective of Stalin and Soviet Union. This constant defensive entrenchment of the utopia was all the more necessary owing to the political context of the Cold War, whereby the worldmaking projects were heavily contested by a range of global and local actors. Historically home to a sizeable Christian community, the clergy in Kerala had consistently opposed the Soviet Union from the 1920s onwards.82 With the ascent of the communist party to power and their education bills threatening the Christian management of private educational institutions, the ecclesiastical community (supported by caste organizations, the Congress and a wide range of actors) engaged in a highly vitriolic campaign named the ‘Liberation Struggle’. Aided by the CIA, their anti-communist rhetoric created a number of narratives highlighting a godless Soviet dystopia.83 As opposed to the transnational solidarities extended to the communists in Russia prevalent in the works of Marxist and other literary intellectuals in Kerala, their works forged global anti-communist solidarities, creating affective communities with the victims of communist rule. Alluding to the death of three people in Angamaly as a result of a police firing during the Liberation Struggle, Deepika, the mouthpiece of the Catholic Church declared,
‘The communist poison tree will not grow in this soil that is wet with life-blood of those martyrs … Their valiant cry is echoing in the horizons, and the horizons tremble. Far far away the cry is being repeated by bones and skills hidden in the soil of Siberia, of Puznan, of Budapest, of Lhasa’84
Thus, multiple worlds of Russia – both utopian and dystopian - collided during the Cold War. The afterlives of the Progressive Literature Movement's contestations over the worldmaking also continued into the 1950s. Members of the erstwhile literary circles of the PLM continued to contest the Russian worlds in the literature of the communists. Kesavadev's ‘Russia's Lover’, for instance, was a response to Gopalan's Soviet narrations. He proclaimed, ‘There are two Russias. One Russia is the Russia of exploiters and the Russia of the exploited. The likes of Gopalan have only highlighted the former Russia.’ Kesavadev then proceeded to exhaustively document the testimonies of defectors highlighting a wide range of human rights abuses and the violence of the Soviet regime including labour camps, concluding, ‘Russia cannot be an example for a people who respect humanity and desire equality and freedom.’85 The developments of the 1950s in the Soviet Union, such as the anti-Pasternak campaign, were also unequivocally condemned by literary associations in Kerala. Numerous newspaper dailies also entrenched a Soviet dystopia marked by the absence of freedom, enabled by the prevalent Cold War narratives of Russian totalitarianism.86
The late-Stalinist era, dating from the end of the Cold War to the death of Stalin, is regarded as the most isolated period of Soviet history. After his death in 1953, his successors brought an end to this isolationism. Rather than the previous Comintern mode of spreading revolution across the world, the new internationalism of the Soviet Union amalgamated the ideas of socialist internationalism of the 1920s with the cultural internationalism of the 1950s. This has to be understood against the bipolar context of the Cold War where both the US and the Soviet fought for hegemony, seeking to present their societies as models for the rapidly decolonizing Third World. Culture constituted a major site of this internationalism, and cultural artefacts were deployed to mobilise both external and internal audiences.87 Translations of Soviet literature were also generated and circulated across the world as tools of this Soviet internationalism.
According to Benoy, the election of the Communist party to power in Kerala in 1957 resulted in a central position for Kerala in Soviet cultural policies. From 1966 onwards, the Soviet government decided to translate Russian works to Malayalam. To do so, a separate division was set up by Progress publishers, which traced its origins to 1931. Progress publishers brought a number of translators from Kerala. The most famous and prolific of them were the couple Gopalakrishnan and Omana. Together, the couple translated almost 200 works to Malayalam, especially belonging to children's literature. In 1982, a separate division called Raduga Publications was established for children's literature. These works were distributed in Kerala by Prabhath Book House which was registered in 1952. They often organized book fairs and exhibitions to sell books printed in the Soviet Union.88 While dialectical worldmaking interventions during the previous period certainly continued during this era through the translations of Progress publications, the Soviet story worlds within this translated children's literature in Kerala added another stratum to the communist internationalism. These hardbound books, with beautiful and colourful illustrations, became highly popular and influential across Kerala.89
Endowed with a heavy subsidy from the Soviet Union, which made them quite affordable, these works were circulated across a staggering distribution network spanning towns and even remote villages. They were read voraciously in a wide range of spaces including houses, schools, libraries and barbershops.90 These stories continued the future-present function of the socialist realist project, albeit in a different context of Cold -War propaganda. The ’future’ was the ‘present’ Soviet Union. Maxim Gorky, a canonical figure of socialist realism, spoke at length about the representation of the past, present and the future in literature. While the past and present have been experienced, he argued, ‘We must also know a third reality – the reality of the future. We must somehow include this reality in our everyday lives, we must depict it. Without it we will not understand what the method of socialist realism.’ Socialist realism produced this future reality through aestheticization by imagining the ‘future as the present’ exemplified in its ‘novels about enthusiasm for production, poems about joyous labour, films about happy life, songs and paintings about the wealth of the Soviet land’.91This happy present depicted in literature was far removed from the ‘grey workdays, routine daily labour’, and the mundaneness if not oppression that often characterised the lived reality of Stalinist Russia. In fact, many authors themselves turned to children's literature in the late 1920s and 1930s owing to the dictates of ideological correctness in literature elsewhere.92 Many of them were suppressed ruthlessly, including the likes of Daniil Kharms and Alexander Vvedensky, who were arrested, and later died, during the period of the Great Terror.93 Producing verbal and visual substitutes for this repressive reality, Socialist realism was the process through which Soviet reality was transformed into socialism. The dream socialist future thus capsized into the happy present of Soviet novels, songs and poetry.
During the previous periods in Kerala this Soviet future-present found its expression through news reports and translations by party intellectuals. During the Cold War era it was also manifested in Malayalam and English translations of children's literature. See for instance, the glorification of the happy Soviet Union in Chuck and Geck. This was a highly popular children's novel on the adventures of two brothers:
‘Each understood the meaning of happiness in his own way. But one and all knew and understood that they must live honourably, work hard, and love and cherish the vast, happy land known as the Soviet Union.’94
Scholars of children's literature have argued how these stories immerse children in the story worlds enabling them to connect with children across the world.95 This is aided greatly by illustrations which enable personal and empathetic responses to these characters in the story worlds.96 The children of Kerala were thus immersed in these story worlds, adorned with rich and glossy illustrations. The reputed author K R Meera for instance reminisced that these books not only gave her an exposure to another world, but also hit her with the realisation that people are the same everywhere.97 Benoy further notices that the translations often emphasised the similarities between Malayalam and Soviet contexts rather than the differences, facilitating a new mode of situational identification for children.98
As hinted at earlier, most of these stories continued the social realist emphasis on the dignity of joyous labour and the self-esteem of the proletariat. For instance, Baalavaadiyilekku showcases an elaborate and rich description of children building a model house according to a plan, instructed by an architect.99 This exercise concludes affirming the pride of toil, ‘After building a house according to the plan, they loved it. After all, they had built it from their hands themselves.’ While Baalavaadiyilekku featured mostly description of the activities at school, many other stories had a central conflict which was resolved through persistent labour against all odds by the protagonist. Chenkunnu featured a sparrow and his wife finally succeed in building a house against multiple odds, most prominently an antagonistic cat.100 The story Dheeranaya Urumbu was driven by a nameless ‘proletarian ant’ who relentlessly fought against various odds, and built a floating arc as nest.101 The central conflict in Chuck and Geck also is resolved through the productive labour of a mother stranded in a house, waiting for her husband. Many also continued the social realist denunciation of the mystical and the magical. In Chuck and Geck, Chuck for instance proclaimed, ‘All magicians are crooks.’ The story Visiting Grandpa by N Nosov emphasised the final triumph of labour over superstitions and magic.102 The story worlds also entrenched the prevalent gender norms emphasising the reproductive labour of women and the role of mothers, as evidenced by the nameless mother in Chuck and Geck.
However, this situational identification with Russia was quite different from that of the early decades. These stories facilitated an identification with the post-revolution Russia. The ‘future present’ literary function ensured a political pasteurization whereby ‘the energy sparked by the October Revolution gradually became standardized’ which rendered revolution benign without entirely neutering the revolution. The censure of mysticism and the continued glorification of labour in a happy idllyic Soviet Union reflected this pasteurization. Perhaps it is no surprise this pasteurization also favored the context of Kerala's communism which had progressively deradicalized to a form of state-centric parliamentary communism. The incumbent communist parties themselves ruthlessly suppressed the Maoist Naxal agitations.103 It is perhaps no surprise that while Prabhath Book House encouraged the circulation of thousands of copies of these stories, the Communist party did not actively encourage the revolutionary currents of Che Guevara or Mao during the same timeframe of the sixties.104

Conclusion

In this work, I sought to trace the history of communist worldmaking vis-à-vis the Soviet Union, using the literary archive of Kerala. The early days of the post-Bolshevik revolution corresponded to individual idiosyncratic textual engagements and situational identification with the underprivileged in Tsarist Russia. Later, the conjecture of the prominence of communist party formations in Kerala and the progressive literature movement led to a socialist realist internationalism characterised by historical materialist worldmaking, notions of progress as well as various competing and polarised accounts of the Soviet Union and its leaders. These existing literary-political contestations were intensified with the Cold-War era, complemented with new modes of Soviet cultural internationalism, which created new channels of affiliations with Russian story-worlds especially through translated children's literature. These above-mentioned affiliations of Kerala with the Soviet Union were just one of the region's many expressions of communist internationalism, such as the literary connections with Latin American.105
More than half a century has passed after Damodaran's despairing encounter with Pravda. At the time of writing, a communist coalition remains in power in Kerala, winning a landslide victory in 2021. Conducting its annual spectacular International Film Festival of Kerala in the capital city of Trivandrum in 2022, the government celebrated the Iranian filmmaker Mahnaz Mohammadi. Unable to travel due to the prohibition of the Iranian government in the wake of women's protests across the country, she sent a strand of hair to her friend, the Greek filmmaker Athina Rachel Tsangari in response. Tsangiri brandished this strand of hair, eliciting a thunderous applause and standing ovation from the crowd.106 Prem Kumar, the Vice-Chairman of IFFK and a party sympathiser, proclaimed the universality of man, declaring solidarity with the suffering people across the world.107 The collapse of the Soviet Union and the rampant embrace of markets and capital by the neoliberal era communist parties have certainly spelled the end of a revolutionary socialist ‘future’. However, the ‘present’ of an aesthetic semblance of the internationalism of situational identification with the oppressed elsewhere refuses to wither, continuing to persist in traces. This identification with the oppressed elsewhere, unfortunately, lies in stark contrast to the dis-identification of Kerala's mainstream Left with the oppressed nearer home. With the progressive dominance of the upper and middle class/castes within the party and the increasing influx of the conditionalities of neoliberal market mechanisms, the marginalized sections including Dalits and women have witnessed a steady increase in socio-economic exclusion.108 This juxtaposition, occurring against the backdrop of a thriving nostalgia for Russian children's books (prompting widespread digitization of these works across Kerala's social media)perhaps illustrates how Kerala's communist internationalism is confined largely to sentimental and aesthetic terms in the 21st century.109
