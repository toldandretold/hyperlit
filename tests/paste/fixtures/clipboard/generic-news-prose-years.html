The Company That Governed a Subcontinent

In September 1599, a group of merchants met in a half-timbered hall off Moorgate Street and agreed to subscribe thirty thousand pounds toward a voyage east. The charter they received the following year granted them a monopoly on all English trade beyond the Cape of Good Hope, and it is fair to say that none of them understood what they had just been handed.

By 1720 the company employed more clerks in London than the Crown employed civil servants. Its warehouses along the Thames held pepper, saltpetre, indigo and Bengali silk in quantities that moved prices across the whole of northern Europe, and its directors had learned that a well-placed loan to the Treasury bought more security than any number of guns.

The East India Company's directors faced something similar in 1773 when Parliament passed the Regulating Act, an early attempt at state control. In Parliament's case, it intervened to restrain a corporation's violence. What is striking, reading the debates now, is how little anybody expected the arrangement to last.

Burke's speeches on Fox's India Bill run to several hundred pages in the collected edition, and almost none of them are about India. His greatest anxiety was not what the company was doing in Bengal but what its money would do to Britain. The lesson of the East India Company is not only that corporations can behave monstrously abroad. It is that they can flex their powers at home, too.

Something like that anxiety has returned. Gallup found that nearly four in 10 Americans said A.I. did more harm than good, up sharply from a year earlier, and nearly eight in 10 expected it to reduce the number of American jobs. The anger has found its most tangible target in the data centres that are the physical embodiment of the boom.

In the first three months of 2026 alone, organized local opposition blocked or delayed 75 major projects worth some $130 billion, and the same Gallup poll found that more than seven in 10 voters, including large majorities of both parties, said they wanted no such facility built near their homes.

The most sweeping regulatory assault in the history of corporate China followed. Alibaba was fined $2.75 billion, Ant was forcibly restructured, and parallel campaigns were started against whole sectors of the Chinese economy. Mr. Ma, the highest-profile casualty of the crackdown, largely disappeared from public life, resurfacing in February 2025 to shake hands at a televised symposium of chastened entrepreneurs.

Whereas one constellation is a single man's company, China's answer is a state enterprise reporting directly to Beijing, alongside a municipally backed rival. Between them they plan to have over 27,000 satellites in orbit before the decade is out.

Historians have argued since 1968 that the company's collapse was less a matter of scandal than of arithmetic. It had borrowed against revenues it did not yet control, in a currency it could not print, on the security of a monopoly Parliament could revoke at will.

Adam Smith, who devoted a long and irritable section of his 1776 book to the subject, thought the whole arrangement absurd on its face. A company of merchants, he wrote, is incapable of considering itself as a sovereign, and its interest as a sovereign is always subordinate to its interest as a merchant.

That judgement was quoted approvingly in the Commons in 1783, and again in 1813, and again in 1833, each time by men who then voted to renew the charter anyway. The pattern is familiar enough to anyone who has watched a modern legislature question a chief executive.

The company was finally wound up by an act of 1874, three decades after it had ceased to trade in anything at all. By then it existed chiefly as an administrative habit, a set of forms and salaries and pension obligations that nobody had got round to cancelling.

What replaced it was not less power but differently distributed power, and the men who had run the company mostly kept their offices. The transition was so smooth that Calcutta clerks reported noticing no change in their duties from one year to the next.

There is a version of this story in which the moral is about empire, and a version in which it is about corporate form, and the two are harder to separate than either side of the argument usually admits. Both versions end in the same place: with a private body exercising public power, and with the public discovering the fact too late to do much about it.

Whether that is where the present decade ends is not yet settled. But it is worth noticing how much of the vocabulary we reach for — charter, monopoly, franchise, regulation — was invented the last time this happened.
