Enclosure and the Commons

The statutes of the eighteenth century did not invent private property so much as narrow the range of things that could be held in common.[1] What had been a bundle of overlapping claims — grazing, gleaning, estovers, turbary — was progressively resolved into a single owner and a fence.

Contemporaries understood the change in moral rather than economic terms. The pamphlet literature of the period is thick with complaints about idleness, and thick too with the assumption that access to a common was what made idleness affordable.[2]

Modern scholarship has largely abandoned that framing, but it took a surprisingly long time to do so, and the older account still surfaces in general histories.[3] The reasons are partly archival: the records that survive are the records the enclosers kept.

What replaced the common was not simply a field but an administrative apparatus — surveyors, commissioners, awards, and a great deal of litigation.[4]

[1] Thompson, E. P. Customs in Common. London: Merlin Press, 1991, pp. 97-184.

[2] See the discussion of the pamphlet literature in Neeson, J. M. Commoners: Common Right, Enclosure and Social Change in England, 1700-1820.

[3] The persistence of the older account is itself the subject of a substantial literature.

[4] On the commissioners and their awards, see the county studies collected by the Record Society.
