The Long Nineteenth Century of the Telegraph

Submarine cable was the first infrastructure whose capital cost was so far ahead of its revenue that it could only be built by companies with a state guarantee behind them.[1] The pattern set there is still visible in orbital communications today.

The Atlantic cable of 1858 failed within a month, and the failure was instructive: the operators had driven it at voltages high enough to destroy the insulation, on the theory that signal strength was the binding constraint.[2]

By the 1870s the engineering had settled and the politics had not. Landing rights became the choke point, and landing rights were sovereign.[3] Every subsequent communications technology has rediscovered this.

What is striking in the parliamentary record is how quickly the strategic framing displaced the commercial one, and how completely.[4]

Notes

On the guarantee structure and its critics, see the Select Committee report of 1860, which is unusually candid about the arrangement.

The insulation failure is analysed at length in the contemporary engineering press, and the analysis holds up well.

Landing rights are the subject of a large treaty literature that begins in earnest in the 1880s.

The shift is documented in the Colonial Office files, and it happened faster than the commercial parties expected.
