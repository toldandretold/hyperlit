Notes on Standardisation

A standard succeeds when it stops being visible. The shipping container is the usual example, and it is a good one, but it obscures how much of the work was legal rather than mechanical.1 The box itself was trivial; the tariff schedules were not.

Rail gauges tell a similar story in reverse. Britain settled on 4 ft 8½ in through a parliamentary commission that was, by its own account, choosing between two workable options and picking the more widely deployed.2

Where standardisation failed, it usually failed because the parties who would bear the conversion cost were not the parties who would capture the benefit.3 That asymmetry is the whole subject.

The lesson generalises awkwardly. A standard is a coordination device, and coordination devices are exactly the things that markets are worst at producing unaided.4
