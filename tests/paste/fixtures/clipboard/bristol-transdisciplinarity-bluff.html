It’s time to call transdisciplinarity’s bluff

Transdisciplinarity occupies an increasingly prominent space in contemporary academic discourse, often signalled as a pathway to better science. Yet beneath its institutional endorsement and expanding literature lies a persistent conceptual ambiguity and rhetorical repetition. While formal publications continue to engage with transdisciplinarity in overly optimistic tones, scholars and practitioners often reserve their frustrations for hallway chat, coffee-corner gossip, and conversations about the tensions and contradictions of their work during conference breaks.

This provocation aims to surface and articulate the underlying tensions that have shaped the dominant narratives of transdisciplinarity. By revisiting how these narratives gained traction and interrogating the assumptions used to justify transdisciplinarity, it turns empathetic critique into a space for reflection and renewal. I invite fellow scholars to reconsider how we position transdisciplinarity within our knowledge ecosystem, what we expect it to do, how it operates, and how it relates to other frameworks that engage with similar societal and epistemic challenges.

The goal is to move beyond superficial claims and hurried publication cycles towards a more serious, ethically grounded and epistemically rigorous engagement, one that confronts transdisciplinarity’s blind spots and situates it within broader debates on knowledge, power and the moral role of science.

Key messages

  • This provocation seeks to surface tensions within dominant transdisciplinarity narratives, which have drifted from the original propositions and now risk becoming conceptually diluted and rhetorically repetitive.

  • It critiques the misframing of the problem and the superficial proposition of integration, which is often romanticised as inclusive without critically engaging with power dynamics or the epistemic rigour required for meaningful collaboration across knowledge systems.

  • Building on these critiques, the provocation invites scholars to engage with the historical, philosophical and epistemological foundations of transdisciplinarity, encouraging a more grounded and reflective repositioning of its role within our broader knowledge ecosystem.

Lost in transdisciplinarity

In 1979, Joseph J. Kockelmans warned against superficial endorsements of interdisciplinarity by those who failed to grasp its inherent fluidity and the complexity of its diverse manifestations. He argued that such advocates had done much damage to all legitimate claims that could be made about interdisciplinary issues (Kockelmans, 1979: 68). Kockelmans warned of the damage caused by advocates of interdisciplinarity who promoted it in overly simplistic and uncritical ways, turning it into a passing trend. At the same time, the lack of consistent terminology and rigorous definitions contributes to widespread conceptual confusion. He was particularly concerned about the rise of negative generalism. Drawing on Chesterton’s definition of a specialist as someone who knows everything about nothing, Kockelmans warned that interdisciplinarity may produce individuals who, conversely, know nothing about everything (Kockelmans, 1979).

Forty-five years on, despite a dramatic surge in publications on inter- and transdisciplinarity,1 his concerns remain strikingly relevant. Much of the literature still suffers from conceptual ambiguity, rhetorical repetition and disjointed arguments. For every book read another emerges, often saying something but adding little. Yet, institutional endorsement has steadily grown, funding agencies now routinely require them as markers of ‘better science’, and universities and research institutes proudly present their adoption as evidence of doing science differently. This has contributed to the deterioration of transdisciplinarity into little more than a symbolic mascot (Backman et al, forthcoming), with its vocabulary, including terms such as integration, participation, co-creation, co-production and boundary crossing diluted into buzzwords that might cause excitement in specific academic micro-tribes, but that increasingly provoke scepticism or even eye-rolling from those outside these circles who seek clarity and substance.

This surge can be seen as enriching the field by opening space for diverse interpretations, approaches and practices; yet it can also be paralysing, as the complex and at times marginal jargon introduced by academics is often difficult for their audience to decipher. Sometimes, it seems to be an innocent conceptual drifting arising less from deliberate exclusion than from a more subtle, cumulative process, resembling the game of Telephone (a sequential whispering game), in which a message becomes progressively distorted or diluted as it passes from one person to the next.

At the same time, the development of the field has not been immune to tacit politics, academic alliances and subtle biases. Journals, for instance, may privilege familiar voices and narratives, with peer reviewers at times conditioning approval on adherence to particular perspectives and occasionally encouraging the inclusion of certain citations. Edited volumes can unintentionally reinforce particular perspectives through relatively narrow citation networks, while selective referencing or omission may contribute to sustaining established viewpoints (critical reading of Szostak and Smiraglia, 2025).

Frustration with this situation intensifies when we attempt to translate these propositions and ideas into practice, whether in research or education, only to find ourselves piecing together fragments from numerous publications to construct a single coherent sentence. One might view this as an illustration of the integration central to inter- and transdisciplinarity. Yet, I would argue that it is little more than a tiring exercise in paying tribute to scholars who, in many cases, have not done the necessary intellectual work.

This issue has been incisively diagnosed by Morrison in his PhD thesis, where he extensively demonstrates the divides between various traditions of research on interdisciplinarity. These divides include: (1) Historio-Theoretical Research of Interdisciplinarity, which focuses primarily on the question of what interdisciplinarity is; (2) Scholarship of Interdisciplinary Teaching and Learning, which examines the processes, methods and outcomes of specific cases of interdisciplinary teaching and learning; (3) Research on Interdisciplinary Research, which investigates the processes, methods and outcomes of interdisciplinary research itself; and (4) Educational Research on Interdisciplinarity, which explores how interdisciplinarity is positioned within curricula and broader debates on learning and teaching (Morrison, 2015). These foundational divides are equally visible within transdisciplinarity. While each focus is valuable, their excessive fragmentation appears counterintuitive, and even counterproductive, to the spirit of transdisciplinarity, illustrating how easily scholarship can become absorbed in serving micro-tribes rather than confronting the more fundamental question of what makes such work meaningful (Alvesson et al, 2017). In this context, a significant portion of these efforts appears to serve as a means of career advancement or gatekeeping, clearing space for roles such as integration experts whose responsibilities often remain poorly defined or ambiguous. These can be added to Julian Kirchherr’s typology of scholarly ‘bullshit’, which includes boring-question scholarship, literature reviews of literature reviews, recycled research, master’s thesis madness and activist rants (Kirchherr, 2023).

Amid this landscape, a dominant narrative of transdisciplinarity has emerged, popularised by practitioners who engage only marginally with its historical, theoretical and philosophical foundations. This has resulted in an academic discourse saturated with repetitive, uncritical and self-congratulatory narratives that celebrate practice with minimal reflection, often reducing transdisciplinarity to romanticised notions of openness while ignoring persistent power asymmetries within collaborations (Barry and Born, 2013; Lawrence, 2025a). I refer to this trend as pop-transdisciplinarity: a diluted approach that frames integration as a quick fix for complex societal challenges.

For this provocation, my aim is not to map these divides and conceptual drifts, but to draw attention to the problems inherent in the currently dominant and inflated conception of transdisciplinarity, in an effort to open space for more empathic and critical work both on and within the field. To this end, I will first outline the prevailing case made for transdisciplinarity (what problem it addresses and the approach it proposes), then situate it alongside other perspectives that grapple with similar concerns. I will then conclude by interrogating the tokenistic model of inclusivity and initiate a discussion towards a transdisciplinarity grounded in the ethical imperative to learn from and engage with diverse epistemologies so that we may collaborate meaningfully across differences.

The strange case of transdisciplinarity

The problem

A significant portion of the literature advocating transdisciplinarity leans on the tired metaphor of disciplines trapped in their silos or ivory towers, incapable of addressing the complexity of today’s societal challenges (Jacobs, 2014: 18). While rhetorically appealing, this narrative rests on a reductive understanding of disciplines, overlooking their continual evolution, internal reflexivity and organic interactions with one another. More importantly, no work to date has convincingly shown that disciplines are inherently incapable of collaborating to tackle societal problems. Most analyses remain case studies examining transdisciplinary initiatives in particular settings and drawing lessons about what succeeded or failed. Even when such studies demonstrate positive outcomes from transdisciplinary collaboration, they cannot substantiate the claim that disciplines themselves are fundamentally deficient in addressing complex problems.

However, the metaphorical narrative comes with a cost. First, it overlooks the heterogeneity and historical development of disciplines, erasing their complexity and situatedness (Osborne, 2015; Lawrence, 2025a). Second, it misrepresents disciplinary stewardship as mere monopolisation of knowledge. While knowledge monopolies do exist, this framing obscures the critical and reflective practices internal to disciplines, supported by their methodological quality controls, which is precisely the kind of epistemic rigour that transdisciplinary work aspires to but has yet to secure. Third, by defining the problem too simplistically as a problem of disciplinary boundaries, transdisciplinary advocates underplay the role of politics, culture, ideology, epistemic commitment and other entangled factors.

The irony is striking: by reducing disciplines to simplistic caricatures of the problem, transdisciplinarity undermines its own core principle. It fails to exemplify the idea that defining complex problems is not a matter of metaphorical gestures, but a rigorous process that demands careful attention to context and complexity.

The proposition

Not only is the problem that transdisciplinarity seeks to address often vaguely defined, but engaging with the recent literature reveals a persistent struggle to find satisfying definitions for its central concepts. From the very notion of what constitutes a discipline, to the criteria for identifying complex or wicked problems that supposedly necessitate a transdisciplinary approach, to the meaning of integration itself, much remains conceptually unstable.

Many of us have attended, or even delivered, presentations that use these familiar visual diagrams to distinguish between multi-, inter- and transdisciplinarity. Yet these representations often gloss over the deeper epistemological and political questions that underpin these concepts. They tend to settle on the idea of integrating various ways of knowing and doing to solve real-world problems, without critically examining what that integration entails or how it should be enacted.

At first glance, integration appears to be a promising proposition within transdisciplinarity. Yet in practice, it is often reduced to a symbolic gesture, merely pulling more chairs up to the scientific table. This approach continues to struggle with the asymmetrical dynamics between those who set the research agenda and those invited to participate. It overlooks who bears the burden of time, labour and epistemic translation, and who is expected to go out of their way to contribute their ways of knowing and doing.

Rather than challenging dominant systems of knowledge and practice to refine their methods in pursuit of moral accountability, pop-transdisciplinarity often proposes to democratise knowledge. It suggests that all epistemologies are treated as equally valid and that their interaction will naturally yield more comprehensive solutions. This logic closely resembles the distorted use of the invisible hand in market economics, which, as Dellemotte (2009) notes, is invoked as an excuse to spout the most extraordinary platitudes about the benefits of the market economy (Dellemotte, 2009: 28). In this analogy, integration spaces and co-creation platforms become the new invisible hand arenas where diverse interests converge and self-organise into optimal outcomes.

Just as economists stripped Adam Smith’s ideas of their theological and moral foundations (Dellemotte, 2009; Olsson, 2011), pop-transdisciplinarity risks reproducing similar blind spots. Pop-transdisciplinary practices often overlook that different modes of knowing and making are rooted in distinct epistemologies. Instead of engaging these systems in their full complexity and acknowledging the asymmetries between them, they tend to treat knowledge as modular and interchangeable. This mirrors the logic of the market, where participation is mistaken for quality and efficiency, and accessibility becomes the sole moral currency. As a result, much of the discourse on integration within transdisciplinarity remains confined to simplistic notions of inclusivity, failing to confront the deeper structural imbalances it claims to address.

Epistemic panic

The interplay between conceptual drifts and the dynamic governing academic publications has given rise to a dominant narrative within transdisciplinarity, increasingly difficult to challenge and steadily shaping the boundaries of the conversations. Its influence stems less from rigorous argumentation than from its extensive and uncritical presence across academic platforms. Alternative perspectives are often overshadowed, and at times silenced, unless they manage to frame their contributions in ways that align with this prevailing narrative.

Interestingly, the dominant narrative within transdisciplinarity has drifted towards precisely what its foundational project warned against: bypassing critical epistemological debates, embracing reductive models of science–society integration (Apostel et al, 1972: 33–4). It also portrays discomfort with disciplinary boundaries, rather than engaging with disciplines through their dynamic relationship with other disciplines and with the problem of society’s (Apostel et al, 1972: 9). Early works, such as those by Jantsch and Mahan, challenge the idea of science as an autonomous endeavour, insisting it must be critically situated within its broader social purpose (Mahan, 1970; Jantsch, 1972). Pop-transdisciplinarity often collapses these guiding principles into a rhetoric of inclusivity and total democratisation of science. Against this backdrop, Lawrence (2025b) has traced significant transdisciplinary ‘paths’, situating them within their positionalities. In doing so, he rekindled transdisciplinarity with the tradition of thinkers such as Montuori, Morin, Nicolescu and others, whose contributions are habitually marginalised or referenced only in passing rather than explored with the depth they warrant.

Pop-transdisciplinarity risks reinforcing a false autonomy through conceptual dislocation that obscures its relations to both the issues it seeks to address and the alternative propositions grappling with similar concerns. This discursive slippage is symptomatic of a deeper epistemological instability: transdisciplinarity struggles to articulate its own ontological status within the ecology of knowledge production. Is it to be apprehended as an epistemological orientation, a methodological dispositif, an approach, a theoretical paradigm, a scholarly tradition, a discipline in its own right or an anti-disciplinary/undisciplinary rupture?

Such indeterminacy is not merely semantic; it signals a deeper, unresolved question regarding the precise problem transdisciplinarity seeks to address. Situating transdisciplinarity within its historical and contextual development may offer a more grounded understanding of its aims and allow for a more coherent positioning within contemporary knowledge systems.

The idea of transdisciplinarity can be seen as a response and a symptom of what I will call epistemic panic, which was diagnosed by Susantha Goonatilake in his 1986 presentation at the UNESCO symposium on Science and the Boundaries of Knowledge. In his analysis, Goonatilake traces the historical trajectory through which Western intellectual traditions appropriated knowledge from the East and South, systematically stripping it of its cultural, religious and moral embeddedness in order to secularise, neutralise and modernise it. Through this process, scientific systems that had once been situated within specific lifeworlds were rearticulated as universal, context-free and ostensibly value-neutral frameworks (Goonatilake, 1986). This hallmarked of the epistemic architecture of modernity.

However, following the retreat of colonial military power and in the aftermath of the Second World War, these claims to neutrality began to collapse. The so-called value-free systems of Western science increasingly revealed themselves as ideologically driven constructs, ones that not only dictated the terms of legitimate knowledge but also actively suppressed alternative epistemologies. According to Goonatilake, this unresolved tension generated a form of intellectual guilt that surfaced in the growing critiques of Eurocentrism and imperialism embedded within the project of modernity itself (Goonatilake, 1986).

Thus, we can read the emergence of transdisciplinarity as unfolding alongside the rise of critical theory, which was gaining influence through the work of Jürgen Habermas, Axel Honneth, Claus Offe and others. In parallel, the intellectual impact of decolonisation was becoming more visible, following its earlier political struggles, through the writings of Frantz Fanon, Edward Said, Gayatri Spivak and their contemporaries. These developments built on wider political and intellectual debates that had intensified after the Second World War, when the authority of the Western scientific tradition of modernity started to appear less secure in its ability to address questions of science in the service of the moral good, science that contributed to society rather than using its power to exacerbate its problems. This period underscored the broader crisis of modernity: a moment when confidence in universal grand narratives was breaking down (Lyotard), the recognition of paradigm shifts and incommensurability in scientific practice (Kuhn) and the growing awareness of the entanglement of knowledge and power (Foucault).

What began as a project to interrogate the blind spots of modernity gradually drifted into a postmodern discourse preoccupied with linguistic representation, often at the expense of substantive engagement with structural and material concerns (Fox, 1992). Yet, in parallel, the past few decades have witnessed the rise of rich intellectual reservoirs such as Post-Normal Science, Mode 2 Knowledge Production, Decolonial and Indigenous Research Methodologies, and Science and Technology Studies. Alongside these, practice-oriented models like Responsible Research and Innovation, Participatory Action Research, Anticipatory Governance, and Transdisciplinarity have emerged, offering genuine potential to reimagine how science is practised, situated and deserves to be held accountable within society.

Situating transdisciplinarity within this broader intellectual context offers a markedly different perspective from treating it in isolation as a panacea. As Morrison skilfully demonstrates, this idealised reading often rests on an unjustified underdeterminism (Morrison, 2015), overlooking its limitations and failing to account for its interplay with alternative frameworks and the dimensions it leaves unaddressed.

Viewing transdisciplinarity through this contextual lens invites discomfort with the notion of integration as its core value – especially when this framing tends to unconsciously treat what is to be integrated as a collection of isolated good practices from various ways of knowing and doing, rather than recognising them as autonomous, coherent, systematic ways of knowing and doing rooted in distinct philosophical and moral traditions.

One foundational assumption remains largely unchallenged: that doing good science simply means doing it together. This mirrors the neoliberal ideal of celebrating participation, co-creation or transdisciplinary spaces as neutral, inclusive spaces and fails to account for the power-laden nature of knowledge production. While collaboration may be ethically desirable, consensus alone does not guarantee epistemic justice (Ludwig and El-Hani, 2025).

Transdisciplinarity must be understood in relation to both the historical context from which it emerged and the broader intellectual landscape it inhabits. It cannot be meaningfully interpreted in isolation from either. Situating transdisciplinarity within this dual framework enables a more precise understanding of the problems it seeks to address and allows us to more thoughtfully position it within the wider ecosystem of knowledge production and societal engagement.

What now?

The early work in transdisciplinarity may well represent the field at its most promising, offering thoughtful propositions for bridging academic boundaries and incorporating societal experiences. These approaches can be effective in contexts where only minor differences exist between ways of knowing and doing. However, when addressing problems that involve actors with fundamentally different epistemologies and practices, aiming for integration as the primary goal becomes an overly ambitious and often impractical proposition, if not undesirable. In such cases, integration tends to reduce these diverse knowledge systems to mere add-ons accepted only to the extent that they conform to the logic and values of the dominant system.

Engaging with the work of Goonatilake highlights the need for contemporary intellectual discourse to reconnect with the origins of the knowledge systems it draws upon, in order to break the cycle of uncritical reliance on the residues of claims from modern science. However, this reconnection cannot be achieved through the lazy act of convening dialogue tables. A genuine exercise in learning from other epistemic traditions is a necessary prerequisite for meaningful transdisciplinary work. This process serves, first, as a form of reparative engagement with knowledge systems historically appropriated from the East and the Global South. Second, it places the responsibility of epistemic labour on Western institutions and scholars, requiring them to undertake the moral and intellectual effort to learn from these traditions, rather than passively expecting those few Eastern voices who gain visibility to educate.

Moving beyond the superficial gesture of simply pulling chairs to the table requires a serious engagement with the question of what constitutes good science, a question that has long preoccupied philosophers and scholars. Thinkers such as Ibn Sīnā (Avicenna), Al-Ghazālī, Ibn Rushd (the commentator) and Ibn al-Haytham grappled with the relationship between metaphysics, theology and empirical inquiry, laying conceptual groundwork for what would later become core concerns in transdisciplinary research, namely connecting and integrating the various ways of knowing. Ibn Sīnā’s synthesis of Aristotelian logic and Neoplatonic cosmology offered a layered framework for understanding reality, bridging essence and existence in ways that supported both scientific inquiry and theological reflection. Al-Ghazālī, in contrast, warned against the overreach of speculative reason, advocating a balance between rational inference and spiritual insight, an early articulation of epistemic pluralism. Ibn Rushd responded by defending the compatibility of reason and revelation, arguing that philosophical and empirical methods could jointly illuminate divine order. These were not peripheral musings but serious philosophical engagements with how knowledge is structured, justified and reconciled across domains. Yet despite their profound contributions, these thinkers were later appropriated by or sidelined in Western epistemology. Their ideas were often reduced to fragments that fit dominant narratives, while the systems of thought they represented were ignored.

Differently, Enlightenment and post-Enlightenment figures like Hume, Kant and Nietzsche interrogated the limits of reason, the conditions of experience and the entanglement of truth with power, conversations that resonate deeply with transdisciplinarity’s concern for epistemic pluralism and reflexivity.

Yet, in contemporary discourse, these rich intellectual traditions have often been sidelined in favour of a more procedural emphasis on the democratisation of science as a quick fix to the problem of the variety of ways of knowing. While inclusion and participation are vital, the shift towards dialogue tables and stakeholder engagement risks flattening the deeper ontological and epistemological tensions that these earlier thinkers took seriously. Recovering these foundational debates may offer a more robust and historically informed basis for rethinking what transdisciplinarity can, and should, be.

In this provocation, I interrogated the dominant mechanistic use of transdisciplinarity, one that treats inclusion as a sufficient gesture, by advocating for a deeper engagement with the social systems involved. This perspective resonates with thinkers like Peter Winch, who, drawing on Wittgenstein’s philosophy, calls for a return to the philosophical foundations of social science (Winch, 1958).

To move beyond pop-transdisciplinarity as a gatekeeping mechanism that preserves the residue of modernity, we must stop treating alternative epistemologies as mere add-ons and begin engaging them as full, coherent systems of thought. This superficial inclusion not only limits the transformative potential of transdisciplinary work but also mirrors broader patterns of symbolic recognition without structural change. This empathic provocation on transdisciplinarity echoes Odetalah’s concern that ‘giving voice to the colonised has lost its revolutionary significance, becoming a carnival celebrating Indigenous peoples within diversity narratives. Their struggles transform into legal issues and minority rights discourse, protected by the same colonial system’(Odetalah, 2013).

Note

1

This transition from inter- to transdisciplinarity is not intended to diminish the considerable distinctions between the two notions that have been well established in academic discussions. Rather, it serves to highlight that transdisciplinarity is encountering many of the same challenges and blind spots that interdisciplinarity has faced in scholarly conversations.
