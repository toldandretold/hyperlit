# Open Scholarship and the Reproducibility Conversation: A Toy Review

## Introduction

Concerns about the reliability of published research have grown steadily over the past two decades. A landmark argument held that most published research findings are false under common conditions of low power and flexible analysis (Ioannidis, 2005). Large-scale replication efforts gave the debate an empirical footing when a coordinated project replicated 100 psychology studies and found that replication effect sizes were about half the magnitude of the originals (Open Science Collaboration, 2015).

The scale of the scientific enterprise compounds the problem. Bornmann (2015) estimated that the volume of published science doubles roughly every 9 years, and surveys suggest questionable research practices are widespread; one meta-analysis found that about 2 percent of scientists admitted to having fabricated or falsified data at least once (Fanelli, 2009).

## Infrastructure and incentives

Several strands of work argue that the infrastructure of scholarly communication shapes research integrity. The movement for open access has long claimed that openness improves scrutiny and reuse (Suber, 2012). Registered reports, which review protocols before results are known, were proposed as a structural fix to publication bias (Chambers, 2013). Preregistration more broadly has been defended as a way to distinguish confirmatory from exploratory analysis (Nosek, 2018).

Citation practices themselves carry integrity risks. Simkin and Roychowdhury (2003) famously argued from citation-error propagation that only about 20 percent of citers read the original paper they cite. Retractions, meanwhile, have increased in absolute terms, and misconduct accounts for the majority of them (Fang, 2012).

## Measurement

Bibliometric infrastructure conditions what can be audited. The launch of large open scholarly graphs made citation auditing tractable at scale (Priem, 2022). Text-mining studies of the literature suggest that statistical reporting errors are common; an automated scan found that roughly half of psychology papers contained at least one inconsistent p-value (Nuijten, 2016).

## Conclusion

The reproducibility conversation has moved from diagnosis toward infrastructure. Whether tooling can reduce the burden on editors and reviewers is an empirical question, and answering it requires exactly the kind of per-citation audit data this toy corpus exists to generate.
