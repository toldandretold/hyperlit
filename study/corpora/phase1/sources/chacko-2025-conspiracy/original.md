## Abstract

Conspiracy theories are increasingly important features of international politics. This article explores the drivers and consequences of states' use of transnational populist conspiracy theories, focussing on India. Through cases analysing the targeting of foreign-funded NGOs, Sikh activists (deemed Khalistanis), and George Soros and the Open Society Foundation, it is argued that transnational conspiracy theories are key tools through which authoritarian populist regimes rearticulate political cleavages, deepen autocratic state power domestically and extend it transnationally. To explain the timing of states' resort to conspiracy theories, this article draws attention to the role of conjunctural conditions – namely, transnationalised political-economic challenges, dissent and criticism – in driving and changing populist articulations. To understand the implications of transnational populist conspiracy theories, this article highlights how they facilitate the legal, virtual, physical and symbolic repression of transnational dissidents and critics; the spread of illiberal and autocratic norms; and the growth of global far-right networks.

Conspiracy theories – stories about secret plots pursued for malevolent ends by powerful actors – have become an increasingly important feature of international politics. Research on conspiracy theories, however, has so far focussed mainly on their role in promoting polarisation and political mobilisation in a domestic sphere (Beller, 2017; Loureiro, 2023; Plenta, 2020; Wojczewski, 2022). Yet, nascent work suggests conspiracy theories can have significant transnational effects. Donald Trump's Covid conspiracy theory blaming China for the pandemic worsened the bilateral relationship and negatively impacted the world's ability to deal with a global crisis (Jaworsky and Qiaoan, 2021). Russian anti-Western and anti-George Soros conspiracy theories have spurred mobilisation dynamics in the Russia-Ukraine conflict (Gentile and Kragh, 2022). Building on this work, and extending its geographical reach – which has primarily been Russia, the United States, Europe and the Middle East – by focussing on India, this article seeks to explain the timing and impact of states' use of transnational populist conspiracy theories. It argues that conspiracy theories are key tools through which authoritarian populist regimes rearticulate political cleavages when confronted with political and economic challenges, and that they facilitate the deepening of autocratic state power domestically and its extension transnationally.

To explain the timing of states' resort to conspiracy theories, this article utilises a Gramscian approach, which has been influential in analyses of contemporary populism in the global South, in particular (Chacko, 2018; Hart, 2013, 2019; Nilsen et al., 2025; Tuğal, 2021; Yalvaç and Joseph, 2019), because of its twin focus on the structural political-economic and agentic discursive drivers of populism. This approach highlights the role of conjunctural conditions – namely, transnationalised political-economic challenges, dissent and criticism that are 'signified and interpreted' (Hall et al., 1978: 320–322) as crises – in driving and changing populist articulations. To understand the implications of transnational populist conspiracy theories, this article draws on and furthers emerging work on transnational authoritarianism (Glasius, 2023; Tsourapas, 2021) which analyses transnational 'obstructive' (coercive) and 'promotional' (soft power, propagandistic) means of 'authoritarian image management' (Dukalskis, 2021: 40). Populist conspiracy theories – which depict transnational elites as colluding with domestic elites and 'others' to oppress the 'people' – work to discursively create and recreate populist cleavages and legitimise the legal, virtual, physical and symbolic repression of transnational dissidents and critics. They additionally contribute to the spread of illiberal, autocratic norms, which erode accountability, undermine individual autonomy and dignity, and further the growth of global far-right networks. In arguing the latter, this article contributes to emerging work on norm contestation by autocratic states, and recent literature concerning the emergence of a 'reactionary international' promoting 'birth-cultural' nationalist forms of sovereignty (Abrahamsen et al., 2024; Campos, 2023; Flonk and Debre, 2025: 17; Michelsen et al., 2025).

The existing literature on India, populism and foreign policy has concentrated on the domestic implications of the transnational expansion of the 'people' in the foreign policy discourse of Prime Minister Narendra Modi, his personalisation and centralisation of foreign policy making and the construction of India as a Hindu state (Plagemann and Destradi, 2019). These studies have suggested that Modi is less populist in his foreign policy discourse than other leaders (like Recep Tayyip Erdoğan) because he emphasises people-centrism rather than the anti-pluralism and anti-elitism that are the hallmarks of right-wing populism (Destradi et al., 2022). They suggest that, unlike populist leaders such as Trump, Modi lacks a conception of a 'globalist elite' (Wojczewski, 2023: 286), and that Indian diplomats' adoption of the 'Hindutva' worldview of Modi's Bharatiya Janata Party (BJP) is an 'an anti- \[domestic\] elite move' (Huju, 2022: 400). As this article shows, however, populist criticisms of a transnationalised elite have, in fact, shaped Indian foreign policy discourses and practices – broadly defined to include diplomacy, diaspora relations, and engagement with transnational actors like non-governmental organisations (NGOs) and international institutions – from the early years of the Modi government. They have intensified since 2019, with the proliferation of populist conspiracies that depict transnational elites, in collusion with domestic elites and 'others', as a threat to the 'Hindu people', justifying domestic and transnational repression. Though Hindus in India are divided along lines of caste, class, region and language, they have been constructed as an organic unity by upper caste Hindu political leaders since the early twentieth century (Dwivedi and Mohan, 2024). This is a project that the BJP continues through an authoritarian populist politics in which 'Hindus are in danger' ('*Hindu khatre mein'*) from a variety of domestic and transnational threats emanating from religious 'others' and liberal elites.

This article begins by elaborating a conceptual framework. The second part details the features of India's authoritarian populist politics. The third, fourth and fifth parts analyse the transnationalisation of this authoritarian populist politics through cases exploring the targeting of foreign funded non-governmental organisations (NGOs), Sikh activists, (deemed Khalistanis) and Soros and the Open Society Foundation. In doing so, these latter sections trace the political-economic drivers of this targeting and analyse its impact on bilateral relationships, transnational institutions, diasporas, and the spread of far-right links, discourses and tropes.

## Transnational populist conspiracy theories as tools of authoritarian populism

Ernesto Laclau's (2005): 74–111) approach to populism as a logic of political articulation provides a framework for the analysis of the dynamic construction of populist cleavages (Katsambekis, 2022). In this approach, populist discursive mobilisation rests on the construction of an 'equivalential chain', wherein a 'vague feeling of solidarity' is condensed out of diverse forms of social grievance and can be mobilised into a popular subjectivity and collective will through the articulation of an equivalential bond, expressed through context-specific signifiers like the 'marginalised' and the 'common man', between different popular demands. The construction of this 'antagonistic frontier' also involves a simultaneous process of articulation whereby sectoral enemies are unified into an overarching enemy and expressed through context-specific signifiers such as the 'oligarchy', 'ruling elite' or 'liberals'. These processes of articulation are unifying, rather than homogenising, and therefore, contingent, and dynamic. This means that the ability of populist political actors to maintain an antagonistic frontier, and the tactics and strategies to achieve this, will change according to shifting political and economic circumstances (Laclau, 2005: 89). In a globalised world of migrant, financial, civil society, communication and political networks, this has both domestic and transnational drivers and consequences.

Conspiracy theories, and the repressive practices they enable, are conceptualised here as tools of populist political articulation (De Leon et al., 2015). As recent work has demonstrated, '\[p\]opulism and conspiratorial reasoning often walk hand-in-hand', reinforcing a sense of crisis, legitimising populist rule and delegitimising opponents (Castanho Silva et al., 2017: 424; Pirro and Taggart, 2023). Scholars have analysed conspiracy theories as providing the emotionally satisfying transgressive discourses that are key to populism (Wojczewski, 2022), but also as linking cultural issues and diverse material interests, thereby unifying heterogenous socio-economic classes while enabling violence against those who lie outside of the category of the people (Loureiro, 2023). Hence, conspiracy theories have more than just discursive effects but can materially disempower some while empowering others.

Why, however, do populist conspiracy theories become prominent at particular times? Laclau's (2005) post-Marxist discourse-centred framework limits its analytical ability to explain the political-economic drivers of populism and its relation to discursive political mobilisation (Hart, 2013; Yalvaç and Joseph, 2019). His (Laclau, 1977) previous work on the role of class transformations and articulations in the emergence of populism, however, was key to the development of Stuart Hall's (1985) conjunctural approach to populism. Hall (1985) highlighted the ways in which conjunctural political and economic crises rooted in capitalist transformations could be used by populist political actors to harness popular struggles and discontents to legitimise authoritarianism. In such conditions, conspiracy theories that resonate with existing grievances become a potent tool of both political articulation and authoritarianism, 'designed to net whole groups of people' and impute 'guilt when hard evidence is scarce' (Hall, 1988: 24–25). Recent research on conspiratorial beliefs suggests they are driven by a variety of motives that are accentuated in times of uncertainty. These motives include the need for epistemic certainty about existing beliefs, an existential desire for control, and a social need to maintain a positive image of a social group (Douglas et al., 2019). Conspiracy theories pertaining to world events, moreover, are common because the nature of international relations – the prevalence of covert state activities and inter-state relations, the deeply hierarchical nature of the international system, the lack of transparency and accountability in international institutions and the opaqueness of foreign policy decision-making – lends plausibility to conspiratorial understandings of how the world is run (Aistrope and Bleiker, 2018).

Conspiracy theories can have long-term effects on institutions, particularly if authoritarianism deepens. Their promotion in institutions and by political leaders, regardless of whether they are genuinely believed, can lead to a conspiratorial world view which may influence threat perceptions, act as a binding constraint in foreign policy actions, and limit the potential for self-correction (De Sá Guimarães et al., 2023; Kragh et al., 2022: 361–362). Hall (1985) noted that while authoritarian populist regimes may achieve dominance, they do not become hegemonic because they cannot address structural economic problems or substantially ameliorate the effects of economic crises. Moreover, as Poulantzas (2014): 195) argued, autocratisation leads to the 'strengthening-weakening' of the state because as it becomes less accountable, and more exclusionary, it increasingly becomes the target and site of political struggles. This provides an incentive for political leaders to use conspiracy theories as a mode of repression.

In a globalised world, authoritarianism, understood here following Glasius (2023) as 'accountability sabotage' – practices by power-holders that disable voice and access to information – involve interconnected state and non-state actors and occur at the national and transnational levels. Work on global authoritarianism draws attention to the transnational practices utilised by autocratic states to both legitimise their regimes and repress criticism (Dukalskis, 2021; Tsourapas, 2021). This work highlights complementary transnational 'obstructive' and 'promotional' means of 'authoritarian image management' (Dukalskis, 2021: 40) involving strategies of legitimation (such as promotional messaging through public diplomacy), co-optation (like patronage of diaspora members), cooperation (such as policy coordination with other states), and repression (such as the harassment of diaspora dissidents and censorship) (Moss and Furstenberg, 2024; Tsourapas, 2021). Moreover, as recent literature on the global far-right indicates, autocratising states with far-right leaderships cooperate to form networks, borrow discourses, and learn from each other's practices (Abrahamsen et al., 2024). This demonstrates the co-constitution of far-right regimes and the interconnected transnational political and economic processes that shape them (Campos, 2023). Bridging global populism, authoritarianism, and far-right studies, this article argues, through analysis of the case of India, that transnational populist conspiracy theories can play a key role in authoritarian image management, facilitating repression, co-optation, cooperation and legitimation, and extending autocratic state power.

## Authoritarian populism in India

Autocratisation in India preceded the election of the Modi regime in 2014. Data from the Varieties of Democracy (V-Dem) Institute shows a decline in India's polyarchy score from 2008 under the Congress Party-led United Progressive Alliance (UPA) with decreases in indices measuring both liberal democracy and electoral democracy (Widmalm, 2019a, 2019b). During the second term of the UPA (2009-2014), there emerged a crisis of accumulation and political legitimacy. While India experienced rapid economic growth in the 2000s, this growth was largely 'jobless', since it did not entail an expansion of manufacturing, and produced a business-state alliance that resulted in problems such as heavily indebted banking and corporate sectors and corruption scandals. Elected on a platform of 'inclusive growth', the UPA's promised expansion of social welfare policies was slow, contested, piecemeal and limited (Chacko, 2018; Nilsen, 2018). Its extractivist growth strategy generated resistance from the Maoist Naxalist movement which was declared to be India's greatest internal security threat by the Congress Prime Minister, Manmohan Singh in 2006 (Rediff, 2006). The movement was led by Adivasis (tribal groups) in central India whose land possessed mineral wealth that became a target of numerous memorandums of understanding between the UPA government and Indian and international mining companies (Shah, 2024). In 2008 and 2013 the government strengthened an already draconian anti-terror law, the Unlawful Activities Prevention Act (UAPA) while the National Investigative Authority (NIA) was placed under the control of the Ministry of Home Affairs and given sweeping new powers. While these amendments were justified as a response to the 2008 Mumbai terrorist attacks, they helped introduce 'a system of impunity under the garb of legality' (Suresh, 2019: 212). The UAPA became an important tool against the members of the Naxalite movement and the activists that campaigned against government abuses (Shah, 2024). The UPA government also faced resistance to attempts to expand reliance on nuclear energy through partnerships with international companies, and genetically modified (GM) food crops with the involvement of Indian biotechnology companies (Chandra, 2020; Newell, 2007). As growth began to slow, Singh blamed foreign-funded NGOs for stoking anti-nuclear and anti-GM protests. In 2011, using an amended Foreign Contribution Regulation Act (FCRA), the government cancelled the licences of more than 4000 NGOs, preventing them from receiving foreign funding. In 2011, as major corruption scandals emerged, an NGO-led India Against Corruption movement protested the government's weak anti-corruption legislation. This drew support from the middle classes, corporate media, business and the political opposition including the BJP and the Hindutva movement (Chacko, 2018).

The BJP was elected to power in 2014, riding a strong wave of anti-incumbency, promising rapid economic development, jobs and efficient governance, and utilised a populist campaign which portrayed the governing Congress Party as corrupt elites who pandered to the Muslim minority (Chacko, 2018). India's democracy indices have steeply and rapidly declined under the Modi regime. According to the V-Dem Institute's 2024 Democracy Report, India is one of the top 10 stand-alone (formerly stable) autocratisers and has been an electoral autocracy since 2018 (Nord et al., 2024: 24–25). The Modi regime introduced further draconian amendments to the UAPA in 2019 and increasingly uses this legislation and the NIA to repress dissent (Suresh et al., 2022). This authoritarian statism reflects an intensified neoliberal economic agenda which is paired with the BJP's Hindutva politics, because both these projects are inclined towards authoritarianism (Jaffrelot, 2010: 207–225; Tansel, 2017).

The intersocial co-constitution of far-right movements has old roots. In the case of India, Hindutva combines European organicist philosophies and concepts from neo-Vedantic reformist Hinduism that emerged during the colonial era. Hindutva leaders (and other radical nationalists, like Subhash Chandra Bose) subsequently forged links with Nazis and Italian Fascists in the inter-war period (Casolari, 2020). Hindutva ideologues, like VD Savarkar, constructed India as an organism under siege from both internal 'viruses', namely, non-violent Buddhism and caste divisions, and external invaders including Central Asians, Muslims and Christians (Mishra, 2022; Savarkar, 1971). Others, like MS Golwalkar and Deendayal Upadhyaya, conceived the Hindu nation in terms of the Vedic concept of 'Virat Purusha' (Cosmic Man), with a Hindu soul (chitti) and 'four castes. . .thought of as analogous to the different limbs of the Virat-Purusha. . .the Brahmins were created from the head of the Virat-Purusha, the Kshatriyas from his hands, the Vaishyas from his abdomen and the Sudras from his legs' (Golwalkar, 1968: 47–48; Upadhyaya, 2008: 1125). In this organic social order, the cosmologically ordained performance of duties is privileged over interests and rights, and social and political divisions are considered pathological. Modi reproduces these ideas, arguing that India has wasted 75 years 'fighting for rights', urging the 'discharging of duties in the next 25 years', and promoting trickle-down development which is to be generated by capitalists, managers and workers operating 'as a family' (Modi, 2022; Quoted in Mukul, 2024).

This organicist conception of the nation means that Hindutva advocates often resort to conspiratorial thinking to explain social and political division and have long also utilised populist strategies, articulating political opponents and minorities against Hindus in its politics. Savarkar viewed Muslims as engaged in transnational conspiracies to take over India, abetted by the Congress Party leadership (Bakhle, 2024: 127–157). He also blamed British colonial rulers and political opponents for delivering concessions to Muslims via a politics of liberal bargaining and 'pampering', disadvantaging Hindus (Savarkar, 2016: 68; Visana, 2023: 169;). Likewise, the BJP leader LK Advani's speeches on 'minorityism' and 'pseudosecularism' in the 1980s accused the Congress government of pandering to Muslims (Fineman, 1991; Jaffrelot, 2010: 214). The Modi regime has utilised populist political strategies to articulate a cross-class and cross-caste Hindu 'people' privileging upper caste forms of Hinduism and capitalist aspiration – captured by the slogan 'Viksit Bharat' (developed India) (Chacko, 2018; Kaur, 2020; Modi, 2024c; Natrajan, 2022). It has simultaneously constructed an antagonistic frontier against 'anti-national' secular, liberal-left elites who are articulated with other religious communities, particularly Muslims. The latter are portrayed as violent, dangerous and disloyal enemies because they adhere to inferior, intolerant religions and ideologies originating outside of India and were responsible for enslaving Hindus prior to the British (Economic Times, 2016; Modi, 2014; Paniyadi, 2016; Rakesh and Mishra, 2016).

While this rhetoric mostly took 'dog whistle' forms in Modi's first term (2014-2019), it intensified in its second term (2019-2024), spurred by intensifying political and economic crises linked to the Covid pandemic and farmers' protests. So-called elites – opposition leaders, university students, activists, intellectuals, bureaucrats, independent journalists – were accused of being anti-national and corrupt by hoarding resources, and 'appeasing' Muslims to use them as a 'vote bank' to gain power, enabling terrorism, and the transfer of Hindus' wealth and entitlements to them (Modi, 2024a, 2024b; Shah, 2024). Modi fashioned himself as a representative of God with divine origins who embodies the common people and was sent to rescue the poor and restore India's (Hindu) unity and civilisational greatness (News18, 2024; Modi, 2019, 2023). Laws restricting access to citizenship and prohibiting religious conversion and interreligious marriage have institutionalised anti-Muslim and Christian discrimination. Vigilante groups linked to the RSS have perpetrated violence against minorities accused of threatening Hindus or violating upper caste social norms (Nielsen, 2023). Federalism and judicial autonomy has weakened, and political authority has been centralised in the Prime Minister's Office and Home Ministry, run by Modi's close ally, Amit Shah, who has used investigative agencies and laws like the UAPA against the regime's opponents (Hansen and Roy, 2022; Mukherji, 2020). Conspiracy theories have been used to justify these discriminatory laws and vigilantism. The 'land jihad' conspiracy, for instance, alleges that Muslims are illegally building shrines on public land and Muslim illegal immigrants are forcing Hindus to sell their land through sabotage. The 'love jihad' conspiracy theory claims that Muslim men conspire to seduce and convert Hindu and Christian women to Islam to demographically dominate India (Arnimesh, 2023).

The regime's 'accumulation strategy' (Jessop, 2013) to foster economic growth also has autocratising implications. Though the BJP once opposed economic liberalisation, since the late 1990s, it generally advanced a neoliberal policy agenda of profitability through the transfer of wealth and resources to the private sector and privileged classes, while inculcating market values in everyday life (Chacko, 2019). In the last few years, following global trends, the government has introduced various protectionist measures, subsidies and assistance to Indian companies, while also initiating new privatisation drives and weakening labour laws (Chacko, 2023). This accumulation strategy involves the cultivation of national and global 'champions' with select conglomerates receiving government contracts and support for their domestic and transnational expansion, including through incentives, favourable regulatory regimes and protectionism (Bharti et al., 2024; Chacko, 2023). This strategy has increased inequality, shrunk market competition and led to industrial concentration, while producing few jobs and intensifying the 'exclusive growth' pattern favouring those with capital and skills that has been the hallmark of India's post-reform economy (Bardhan, 2022: 178; Ghose, 2023). India has been ranked 10th in the *Economist's* crony-capitalist index, with wealth from rent-seeking crony sectors rising from 5% to 8% of its Gross Domestic Product (GDP) over the past decade (Economist, 2023). Far from being unique to India, cronyism has been a feature of the neoliberal drive for competitiveness and market reform of the state (Reinsberg et al., 2021). In the Indian case, cronyism in the neoliberal era has been associated with the liberalisation of the land and resource sectors (Levien, 2021). Cronyism is both a product of, and facilitates, complementary state and 'corporate authoritarian practices' (Glasius, 2023: 126) entailing the undermining of accountability through the persistence of rents, the suppression of political competition in favour of established elites, poor transparency, weak rule of law and compromised regulatory institutions (Enderwick, 2005). In the Indian case, the Modi regime's centralisation of power allows it to favour crony capitalists from his state of Gujarat, namely, Gautam Adani and Mukesh Ambani, tailoring and weakening regulations to suit their Adani and Reliance conglomerates, effectively providing sovereign guarantees to domestic and foreign investors and financial institutions on their behalf, and contributing to the problem of bad loans in India's public sector banks (Bardhan, 2022: 178).

As the remainder of this article shows, this process of domestic autocratisation has had transnational implications. Specifically, as the Modi regime has faced greater resistance, political and economic crises have been signified and interpreted through transnationalised populist conspiracy theories, articulating populist cleavages and extending autocracy domestically and transnationally.

## NGOs

### Crisis

In the last three decades, more than 130 states have undertaken the administrative or violent repression of NGOs, increasingly undermining their role in global governance (Chaudhry, 2022: 2–3). In the case of India under the Modi regime, the administrative repression of foreign-funded NGOs that are seen as obstacles to economic reform, and assertions of state power against them, has been facilitated through populist discourses. Winning populist consent for this authoritarian practice is possible because of long-standing elite and popular grievances about colonial and neo-colonial interference in India. Western philanthropic, NGO and multilateral activities have long been critiqued from a variety of perspectives. The autocratising left-wing populist Congress government of Indira Gandhi introduced the Foreign Contribution (Regulation) Bill in 1973 to restrict foreign funding for political parties. This legislation was initiated amid widespread reports of foreign and domestic interference by American intelligence and multinational companies, including by Daniel Patrick Moynihan, United States (US) Ambassador to India from 1973 to 1975, who alleged covert US funding of Indian politics (including to the Congress Party) (McGarr, 2024: 215–220). In the debate on the Bill in 1976, which was passed when India was under Emergency rule, speakers raised the 'sordid activities' of the World Bank in Chile, the International Monetary Fund in Egypt and the Lockheed bribery scandal (Rajya Sabha, 1973).

As the economy liberalised in the late 1980s and NGOs grew in influence and presence in the 1990s and 2000s, left-liberals critiqued professionalised NGOs for providing lucrative careers to the middle-classes while being complicit in upholding the neoliberal status quo (Srinivas, 2022: 12–15). By the 2010s, as mentioned above, initiatives for nuclear power and GM food faced increasing protest, leading the Congress-led UPA government to critique foreign-funded NGOs for not appreciating the 'developmental challenges that our country faces' (Singh quoted in Bagla and Stone, 2012). While in the 1970s the FCRA targeted funding for political parties, which posed the main opposition to the Indira Gandhi regime, the Congress government's FCRA Rules in 2011 applied to any organisation engaged in political activities and protests (GOI, 2011). The UN Special Rapporteur on the Rights to Freedom of Peaceful Assembly and of Association found the UPA's amendments contravened the International Covenant on Civil and Political Rights (Kiai, 2016). The amendments reflected the post-economic reform context in which NGOs became a major source of opposition to the UPA government's economic policies and the major critics of government corruption. They indicated a tilt towards coercion in the internal balance of hegemony (Chacko, 2018: 13).

### Conspiracy and transnational authoritarianism

Further amendments to the FCRA Rules by the Modi regime and their extensive use against transnational and Indian NGOs with foreign funding reflects a further shift in the balance of hegemony in the context of ongoing political and economic crises. The regime has attempted to legitimise its repression by deploying the populist articulation strategy Modi deployed during his Chief Ministership of Gujarat where activists investigated and contested his autocratising government's role in anti-Muslim violence, extrajudicial killings, dam construction and crony capitalism (Jaffrelot, 2024). In 2006, for instance, he accused NGOs of getting away with a 'vicious cycle' of foreign 'finance-activity-award', 'no matter how many failings of the awardee' (Mody, 2016). In a 2010 speech, Modi (2010) questioned the 'transparency and primacy' of NGOs, and suggested 'middlemen and vested interests' were using the Right to Information (RTI) Act to harass administrators.

As Prime Minister, his regime has utilised conspiracy theories to justify legal, institutional and material strategies that work to undermine foreign-funded NGOs. Using a populist articulation strategy of identifying himself with the poor and domestic foreign-funded NGOs as threatening and arrogant elites, Modi asserted in a speech to farmers in 2016:

> You would have seen that morning to night, I am being attacked . . . First, they don't understand how Modi became a PM, how a chai wala (tea-seller) became a PM of the country; they can't swallow that . . . They are also upset because I told a few NGOs to give us an account of the foreign funds that they spend here. They ganged up and said "beat Modi, beat Modi". . . (Mohanty, 2016)

In 2015 an Intelligence Bureau (IB) report that reproduced portions of Modi's 2006 speech was leaked to the media. The report articulated transnational NGOs including Greenpeace and Amnesty International into a common antagonist of the people, blaming them of reducing India's economic growth by 2% to 3% by stalling development projects, and detailing links between Indian scholars and activists and transnational NGO networks (Intelligence Bureau, 2014).

In the same year (2015), the government froze NGO bank accounts, placed Greenpeace on a watchlist and initiated an investigation into the Centre for the Promotion of Social Concerns, accusing these groups of 'using foreign funds for anti-national activities' (GOI, 2015). By 2016 about 20,000 NGOs had lost their licences for allegedly violating the FCRA and by 2018, foreign contributions to NGOs had dropped by 40% (Swart, 2020). Since 2019, NGO repression has intensified, justified by conspiracy theories alleging they are causing social divisions. Major transnational NGOs like World Vision and Greenpeace have effectively ceased working in India. Many NGO FCRA licences have been suspended for the misuse of funds to disrupt harmony between communities by promoting religious conversion (Bennett, 2017; Indian Express, 2024; Jain, 2021; Kumar, 2024a). In most of these cases the complainant was an NGO ideologically-aligned to the RSS, the Legal Rights Protection Forum, which claims that transnational NGOs like Caritas work in mineral-rich areas for the purposes of 'monitoring the utilization of mineral resources and creating social disharmony through religious conversions into Christianity' (Tiwary, 2024). Amendments to the FCRA in 2020 to limit administrative spending and stop the transfer of foreign funds to other NGOs delayed the delivery of medical aid by major transnational NGOs during the Covid pandemic (Kotecha, 2021). Foreign donors were instead asked to contribute to the government's Prime Minister's Citizen Assistance and Relief in Emergency Situations (PM-CARES) Fund, which was exempt from FCRA provisions and set up as a charitable trust to evade RTI investigations and public audits. This means donors, donations and their use remains opaque (Agarwal, 2020). In 2022, the government also sought to restrict the domestic fundraising of transnational service NGOs, like Save the Children and Sightsavers. The government claimed their campaigns to raise money for addressing malnutrition and blindness were spreading false information and were 'against the spirit' of its own programmes (Tapasya, 2022). By 2024, more than 35,000 FCRA licences had been cancelled or not renewed for reasons including 'anti-national' activities and links, inciting protests and religious conversion (Dasgupta, 2024).

The Modi regime has also utilised an increasingly prevalent form of transnational repression, mobility control (limiting the ability of critics to travel), to curtail transnational NGO activities (Slipwitz et al., 2024). In 2015, a British Greenpeace employee was denied entry to India and an Indian Greenpeace employee, Priya Pillai, was prevented from leaving India to travel to London at the request of the IB (Siddique, 2015). The government claimed Pillai's planned testimony in the British Parliament would negatively impact India's image abroad, contributing to human rights reports which could be used to impose sanctions 'as instruments of foreign policy to impede India's growth prospects at a time when it is actively pursuing economic growth and development, which requires a massive flow of FDI' (Shakdher, 2015).

India's aversion to independent NGOs is also contributing to the co-optation of international institutions to extend autocratic state power by establishing illiberal, autocratic norms. As a member of an unofficial Like-Minded Group in the NGO Committee of the UN Economic and Social Council, which includes other prolific perpetrators of transnational repression like China, Russia and Iran, India now routinely votes against human rights NGO applications for consultative status (Gorokhovskaia and Linzer, 2022: 23). It justifies its approach on the grounds of consensual decision-making, reflecting the organicist anti-politics that characterises India's domestic authoritarian populism (ECOSOC, 2023: 38).

## Khalistanis

### Crisis

The most significant political challenge to the Modi regime has been the 2020-2021 farmers' protests against its agricultural reforms (the 'Farm Bills') which aimed to liberalise and corporatise the agricultural sector. India's rural economy is widely seen as being in an ongoing state of crisis since the 1990s (Walker, 2008). 'Green Revolution' policies introduced in the 1970s to increase crop yields using pesticides, fertilisers and high-yielding seed varieties made India food self-sufficient and created a class of rich capitalist farmers in Punjab and Haryana. Yet, they negatively impacted small-holder farmers and led to long-term environmental damage, ground water and soil nutrient depletion, and greater mechanisation of jobs, resulting in fewer and more precarious jobs for labourers. Economic liberalisation led to imported cheap food and the growth of financial institutions, lessening the reliance on rural capital and lowering government spending on rural development. Subsequently, crop yields stagnated or declined, the cost of inputs for farmers has increased and rural employment has declined, leading to increases in rural poverty and food insecurity (Brown, 2018: 44–48). While agriculture's contribution to GDP continues to decline, the stagnation of the manufacturing sector has meant a lack of growth of urban and non-agricultural employment. Many of these problems have been exacerbated in the past decade because input prices have continued to increase and the Covid pandemic has led to greater reliance on rural employment (Ahmed et al., 2024; Azim Premji University, 2023).

India's agrarian crisis has led to a Gramscian 'crisis of authority' in which active consent from the rural populace has been difficult to maintain (Brown, 2018: 49). Dissent has manifested in various forms of resistance including the Maoist insurgency and protests against special economic zones and land acquisition laws. The 2020-2021 farmers' protests are significant, however, because they were long-running, united a broad cross-class and cross-caste coalition, and targeted the regime's preferred business allies, the Adani and Ambani conglomerates (Lerche, 2021; Nielsen and Nilsen, 2022). The latter stood to gain from the reforms which weakened regulations and restrictions on the sale, production and storage of agricultural goods, including by enabling private sector contract farming and reducing government involvement in the purchase of produce. The protests also mobilised the large Punjab-derived Sikh diaspora in countries like Canada, the United Kingdom (UK) and the US, where they have built political influence (Singh, 2025). While the Farm Bills were eventually stayed by the Supreme Court and later repealed by the government, farmers' grievances remain unaddressed and rural distress has deepened after the Covid pandemic, resulting in new protest actions in 2024 in the states of Punjab and Haryana.

The reforms and the subsequent protests reflected the 'strengthening-weakening' of the Indian state. The Farm Bills were pushed through the Indian parliament in the middle of the Covid pandemic as ordinances, a legacy of colonial governance, which allows governments to pass legislation as an emergency measure, bypassing parliamentary debate and legislative scrutiny. The Modi regime has been a prolific user of ordinances, including for some of its most controversial policies related to demonetisation and citizenship laws, undermining both voice and access to information (Raghuvanshi, 2021). The Farm Bills, like these other major policies, were passed without consultation with stakeholders, including the Chief Ministers of state governments who have remit for agricultural policy in India's federal structure. The Bills and the erosion of federalism were rejected by the then-Congress Chief Minister of Punjab, Amarinder Singh, and by many farmers in the state, which has a majority Sikh population, who protested the reforms for over one year resulting in fatal clashes with the police, Internet shutdowns by the government and disruptions in Punjab, Haryana, and Delhi (Indian Express, 2020). Concurrently, the Modi regime's mishandling of the Covid pandemic led to high death rates, a slowing economy, high inflation and low jobs growth and consumption, leading to a steep decline in Modi's popularity (Biswas, 2021).

### Conspiracy and transnational authoritarianism

In response, the government sought to delegitimise the farmers' protests through a conspiracy linking them to the separatist Khalistan movement, which thrived in northern India in the 1980s and 1990s, but has since largely been dormant. In the 1980s, Sikh mobilisation for the devolution of power was driven by economic dislocation caused by intensive agricultural production and discontent over the central government's agricultural input, procurement and industrial policies. The subsequent manipulation of politics in Punjab by the national Congress government contributed to the emergence of a Sikh militant separatist movement (Chima, 2015: 40–49; Maiorano, 2015: 123–133). This culminated in extensive violence in Punjab, the assassination of Prime Minister Indira Gandhi by her Sikh bodyguards, and the bombing of an Air India flight by Canadian Sikh militants after an anti-Sikh pogrom in Delhi in which Congress politicians and bureaucrats were complicit in the deaths of almost 3000 Sikhs.

Recent concerns about Sikh secessionism have been stoked by the Indian media and the government, despite the lack of evidence of a major new threat (Tur, 2020). A 2018 issue of the prominent newsmagazine *Outlook*, for instance, was devoted to the threat of 'Khalistan II-Made in Canada' and featured stories touting 'A new real threat of Khalistani-terror, fuelled and funded by foreign gurudwaras patronised by liberal white politicians' (Majumdar, 2018). The farmers' protests and the state's response did aid the revival of the Khalistan movement among a small number of Sikhs in Punjab and in the diaspora who, since 2021, have been conducting unofficial referendums on independence. However, a recent Pew Survey found that 93% of Punjabi Sikhs in India were proud to live in the state of Punjab and 95% were proud to be Indian (Pew, 2021). The threat inflation of diaspora-instigated Sikh separatism and the willingness to use force across borders also reflects the worldview of India's national security advisor, Ajit Doval, who spent most of his career in intelligence agencies including in operations against the Khalistan insurgency (Bal, 2023). Doval's doctrine of the 'New India' is consistent with Hindutva precepts that fashion India as a '*viśvaguru'* (teacher of the world) (BJP, 2014: 40): 'We never became aggressors to serve our personal interests. We will surely fight, on our soil as well as on foreign soil, but not for our personal interests. But in the interests of Parmarth \[highest\] spirituality' (Doval quoted in TNN, 2020). While the Hindutva movement often treats Sikhs as members of a sect of Hinduism and, therefore, a part of the 'people', they are sometimes characterised as potentially traitorous 'others' because of the community's colonial collaboration with the British and susceptibility to British divide and rule policies (Moliner, 2020; Visana, 2021: 118).

The protests led to the discursive articulation of new transnational populist antagonists of the people, consisting of Sikh farmers, Indian liberals, diaspora supporters (particularly from Canada which has the largest Sikh diaspora) and supporters among Western celebrities and politicians. These actors were accused of engaging in a conspiracy to undermine India, justifying the introduction of new laws to enable national and transnational digital censorship. The farmers' movement included an 'IT Cell', mimicking the BJP's IT Cell, which acts as a government propaganda arm (Happy and Mogha, 2025). The farmers' IT Cell ran a social media campaign drawing on diaspora Sikh networks and gained support from Rhianna, Greta Thunberg, and Canadian politicians like Defence Minister Harjit Singh Sajjan and Prime Minister Justin Trudeau whose comments on the 'rights of peaceful protestors' and the 'process of dialogue' were described by the Ministry of External Affairs (MEA) as 'unacceptable interference in our internal affairs' (Reuters, 2020).

In a speech in parliament in 2021, Modi claimed the country needed to be protected against a 'new category of protesters' who 'are actually "parjeevi" \[parasites\]. They don't have their own strength, but they join all agitations'. A 'new FDI' he, said had 'come to the fore': 'We need Foreign Direct Investment, but the new FDI is "Foreign Destructive Ideology", we have to protect ourselves from it' (Indian Express, 2021). Articulating the West and dissident emigrants into a common antagonist, a government minister, V.K. Singh, and BJP spokesperson, Gaurav Bhatia, claimed that a PowerPoint protest 'school kit of anarchy' tweeted by Thunberg was evidence of an international conspiracy involving a diaspora Sikh separatist group, the Poetic Justice Foundation, and entities based in India, the UK and Germany (TOI, 2021). Another government minister, Raosaheb Danve claimed of the farmers' protests in the state of Maharashtra: 'The agitation that is going on is not that of farmers. China and Pakistan have a hand behind this' (PTI, 2020). The Minister of External Affairs, S. Jaishankar, described celebrities' comments as '\[m\]otivated campaigns targeting India', and vowed that '\[t\]his India will push back' (Wire, 2021).

This 'push back' involved the misogynistic trolling by the IT Cell of international celebrity supporters and digital transnational repression by the government, both of which aim to disable voice. The government issued hundreds of censorship orders on social media accounts reporting or supporting the protests (Kaur, 2021; Scroll, 2024; Sharma and Srinivasan, 2021). These accounts included those of Sikh diaspora groups, activists and politicians in Canada, the UK and the US. Some activists also alleged Indian government involvement in 'shadow bans' of social media posts (Bhattacharya, 2022; Garcha, 2020). In 2023, the Modi regime blocked Internet access to 27 million people in Punjab as it carried out a search for Amritpal Singh, a separatist leader (Mogul, 2024). It also issued blocking orders on foreign and Sikh diaspora social media accounts, including those of news organisations and Canadian politicians of Sikh heritage who commented on the suspension of civil liberties in Punjab (Johal, 2023). While Twitter/X initially challenged some of these orders, this changed following the introduction of the Information Technology (Intermediary Guidelines and Digital Media Ethics Code) Rules, 2021, which holds the employees of technology companies criminally liable if they do not carry out takedown requests, and after it lost a court case against the government (Mehrotra and Menn, 2023). This regulatory framework has had a global impact on norms of cyber governance, providing a model for online censorship which has been copied by Bangladesh, Nigeria and Myanmar (Mehrotra and Menn, 2023).

Transnational repression via the monitoring and recruitment of diaspora members as informants, including Sikhs, Tamils and Kashmiris, has long been a part of Indian intelligence activities, under successive governments (Anderson and Clibbens, 2018: 1761–1764; Kashmeri, 1985; Legal Tribune Online, 2014; News Agencies, 2020). However, news reports citing intelligence sources suggest the Modi regime developed an aggressive strategy to recruit assets across Europe and Canada aimed at curbing pro-Khalistan activity (Swami, 2020). Several espionage cases involving the surveillance of Sikhs have been tried in German courts, leading to convictions of Indian intelligence assets and the expulsion of an intelligence officer (Office of the Federal Prosecutor, 2019; Federal Public Prosecutor General, 2020; HRR-Strafrecht.de, 2016). While Glasisus (2023): 28) argues that surveillance, if kept secret, does not constitute accountability sabotage, Sikh diaspora often report awareness of being watched and perceive this as intimidation (Dias et al., 2024). Canada's intelligence agency has accused India of more overt practices of intimidation, like the use of diaspora co-optation and repression through blacklists and visa controls. They have also claimed that Indian consular officials have used covert electoral financing to promote pro-India stances, and inference in the last two Canadian elections to fund pro-India candidates and influence politicians (Cooper, 2023; Ling and Boudjikanian, 2024).

Forms of transnational repression involving legal charges, arrest and detention have been facilitated by government claims that protestors were funded and influenced by foreign actors. They have been enabled, moreover, by counter-terrorism legislation linked to multilateral initiatives. India's counter-terrorism laws have been amended to conform to the standards imposed by the transnational Financial Action Task Force (FATF) to curb terror financing and money laundering. These standards influence evaluations that determine access to multilateral loans and investment. They have been criticised for their broad scope and ambiguous terminology which leave them prone to misuse by states to repress dissidents. India has used the need for compliance with FATF standards to justify anti-terror legal provisions that target minorities and dissenters (Chakrabarty, 2023). The broad scope of FATF requirements for filing Suspicious Transaction Reports, and its compliance demands, enabled and legitimised the government's actions (Chakrabarty, 2023: 415–418). Protestors, humanitarian relief, and civil society observer organisations were charged with suspicion of terror financing due to 'abnormal transactions' from the Sikh diaspora groups that organised solidarity protests abroad and are proscribed in India as terrorist groups (Chakrabarty, 2023: 416).

Further, like other autocratic states, such as Russia, India has sought to co-opt Interpol Red and Blue notices (look out notices and requests for information) to return those it considers terrorists to India, without adequate evidence (Bradley, 2024; Firstpost, 2022). British courts have ordered the release of Sikh activists detained for extradition in the UK for extended periods at the Indian government's behest due to insufficient evidence (Tribune, 2021). A British Sikh activist, Jagtar Singh Johal, has been arbitrarily detained in India since 2017 (Wintour, 2022). Contributing further to the co-optation of transnational institutions to strengthen autocratic state power, India, as a member of Interpol's Executive Committee in 2022, voted with China and Turkey against an attempt to have Russia – a prolific user of Interpol Red Notices – suspended from access to Interpol databases (Interpol, 2022).

By 2023, the Modi regime was allegedly carrying out plans to use the most extreme form of transnational repression – assassination – against Sikh separatists in the diaspora. News reports citing anonymous officials suggest assassinations abroad to 'get rid of' enemies and 'send a chilling message' became a part of the government's security policies in 2019. In an example of 'authoritarian learning' (Hall and Ambrosio, 2017), the Indian government apparently took inspiration from Saudi Arabia's murder of journalist Jamal Khashoggi in Turkey in 2018, and targeted pro-Khalistan activists in the diaspora because of the farmers' protests (Ellis-Petersen et al., 2024). In 2020, the government used the amended UAPA to designate nine Sikhs associated with pro-Khalistan organisations as terrorists. The list included Gurpatwant Singh Pannun and Hardeep Singh Nijjar, American and Canadian nationals respectively. In September 2023, the Canadian Prime Minister pre-empted a media leak to announce in Parliament that Canadian intelligence was pursuing credible allegations of Indian government involvement in the killing of Nijjar and had expelled an Indian intelligence officer. In October 2024, Canadian officials accused India of complicity in the murder of Nijjar, and alleged that Indian diplomatic and consular officials instigated a campaign of violence that escalated in 2023 (Tunney and Rafique, 2024). This included using organised crime gangs, including one headed by a gangster, Lawrence Bishnoi, who operates from a jail in Gujarat, to harass, extort, and violently threaten members of the South Asian community (Tunney, 2024b). The Canadian government subsequently expelled six Indian diplomats, including the High Commissioner, and implicated the Indian Home Affairs Minister, Amit Shah, in the crimes (Tunney, 2024a). Canadian officials also claim to have evidence of India's use of cyber-technology to surveil dissidents and attack Canadian government networks (Agence France-Presse, 2024). In December 2023, the US Department of Justice released an indictment accusing an Indian national, Nikhil Gupta, of being involved in a plot directed by an Indian intelligence agent. The agent was identified in an another indictment filed in October 2024, as Vikash Yadav, an officer in India's spy agency, the Research and Intelligence Wing (RAW) which is a wing of Modi's Cabinet Secretariate, to assassinate Pannun by contracting a hitman who was actually an undercover American officer (Singh, 2024). Prior to this, the US government expelled the station head of RAW in San Francisco and blocked the replacement of the station head in Washington DC (Swami, 2023). In 2024, media reports alleged the Australian government had expelled a 'nest of spies' in 2020 who were RAW agents posing as diplomats while monitoring the diaspora (Dias et al., 2024).

Both the Canadian and US governments have condemned India's activities, demanding accountability, coordinating their pressure on India and extending support to Sikh activists (Nakashima et al., 2023; Tunney and Rafique, 2024). Canadian officials have named India as the second most active perpetrator of foreign interference in the country after China (Zimonjic, 2024). The Federal Bureau of Investigations includes India on a list of perpetrators of transnational repression and has extended protection to Pannun, the most prominent of the alleged assassination targets (FBI, 2025). However, the Modi regime's responses to Canadian and American claims have been starkly different, suggesting that populist discourses are used strategically rather than forming the basis of foreign policy and that the transnational extension of autocratic state power is structurally constrained. Towards the US – India's second largest trading partner and its principal geopolitical ally during clashes with China – the regime deployed a 'promotional' strategy designed to depict India as a rule-follower by blaming 'rogue elements' for the Pannun plot, and forming a 'high-powered Enquiry Committee' which eventually released a vague statement recommending charges be laid against an unnamed individual (Wire, 2025). In contrast, India lacks extensive trade or geopolitical links with Canada. They have a history of tension over the Khalistan movement, which has partly been cultivated by Sikh migrants, one of whom was found responsible for the bombing of an Air India flight departing from Canada in 1985. Hence, the Modi regime has been consistent in its use of 'obstructive' strategies, denying Canadian statements that it shared evidence of Indian government involvement in the Nijjar murder, intensifying its transnational repression after the murder allegations were made, promoting disinformation and misinformation, and threatening to remove the diplomatic privileges of Canadian diplomats (a violation of the Vienna convention) (Singh, 2023, 2024). The Trudeau government was further incorporated into the government's transnationalised populist discourse as a corrupt liberal elite actor articulated to diaspora supporters of the Khalistan movement and a one-time political ally, the former New Democrats Sikh-Canadian leader Jagmeet Singh. This collective antagonist was pitted against the representatives of the Indian (Hindu) people in Canada. In a press release, the MEA (2024) attributed Canada's allegations to Trudeau's 'vote bank politics' serving 'the anti-India separatist agenda' by providing 'space to violent extremists and terrorists to harass, threaten and intimidate Indian diplomats and community leaders in Canada'. The government also issued Canadian travel advisories warning of 'politically condoned hate crimes and criminal violence'; described violent clashes outside a Hindu temple and Sikh gurudwara between pro- and anti-Khalistan protestors in Ontario as a 'deliberate attack on a Hindu temple' despite evidence of culpability on both sides; and erroneously alleged Canadian media censorship of Jaishankar (CBC, 2024a, 2024b; News Agencies, 2023; Niranjankumar, 2024). Regardless of the differences in approach to Canada and the US however, the aim and effect in both cases is the undermining of accountability by disabling access to information.

## Soros

### Crisis

Attracting foreign direct investment (FDI) to grow the manufacturing sector has been a cornerstone of the Modi regime's accumulation strategy. Schemes like 'Make in India' and 'Atmanirbhar Bharat Abhiyan' (Self-Reliant India Mission) aim to boost manufacturing through global marketing campaigns. Its policy priorities include improving India's ranking in the World Bank's ease of doing business index, building logistics infrastructure, and offering incentives, such as production subsidies, to boost investment. In his first term, in particular, Modi's frenetic global travels included business delegations and aimed to expand the reach of Indian capital as well as attract FDI to India. Despite these efforts, net inflows of FDI have fallen from 2.4% of GDP in 2020 to 0.8% in 2023 while value-added manufacturing has declined from 16% of GDP in 2016 to 13% in 2023 (World Bank, 2025a, 2025b). Much of the decline in FDI has been the result of capital repatriation by foreign companies, while domestic companies are investing abroad (Raghavan, 2025).

Felman and Subramanian (2024); Subramanian and Felman, 2022) attribute the failure to attract FDI in large part to India's autocratic crony capitalist model. This has created policy uncertainty due to regulatory frameworks being altered to suit favoured conglomerates; protectionism to create 'national champions' (which affects supply chains); and the selective, arbitrary and coercive use of government agencies against businesses. In 2024, for instance, media investigations following a Supreme Court order revealed that 75% of percent of the donations in the government's 'electoral bonds' scheme for political party financing went to the BJP. Thirty corporate donors were being investigated by the Enforcement Directorate and Tax Department for money laundering and tax violations prior to purchasing electoral bonds; and companies donating large amounts to the BJP were awarded major government contracts (Abraham et al., 2024; PTI, 2024).

A key actor in the Modi regime's crony capitalist model, the Adani conglomerate, has rapidly expanded, first in Gujarat and then at the national and global levels, aided by state support and regulatory flexibility. The latter has facilitated corporate authoritarianism, including by weakening environmental regulations; awarding contracts through non-transparent tendering processes; changing rules to facilitate its entry into new sectors; intervening to prevent Adani projects from facing insolvency; providing tax breaks; purchasing electricity from Adani at inflated prices; and selling coal to the company at undervalued prices (Jaffrelot, 2024: 288–291; Rajshekhar, 2019a, 2019b; Saikia, 2022). Between 2014 and 2020, Gautam Adani's net worth increased by 230 percent to more than USD26 billion as his conglomerate won numerous government tenders (Findlay and Lockett, 2020). Adani has also been at the forefront of the government's global economic expansion. Most of its foreign investments have been signed shortly following Modi's diplomatic visits or meetings with other world leaders (Sharma and Tiwari, 2024). An agreement to upgrade Kenya's airport, for instance, was signed after the Kenyan President visited India.

Adani's global expansion has, however, faced resistance. Its Kenya agreement required rule changes in Adani's favour and did not involve a competitive tendering process. This resulted in strikes by airport workers, lawsuits and opposition protests (Herbling et al., 2024). Likewise, a deal to export electricity from India to Bangladesh was signed after Modi's visit in 2015 with little transparency. It resulted in inflated prices and is being reviewed by Bangladesh's new government following the toppling of the autocratic regime of Sheik Hasina (Sharma and Tiwari, 2024; Wire, 2024). In 2023, the *Financial Times'* (*FT*) analysis of customs records led to allegations that Adani was using offshore intermediaries to inflate the price of low-quality coal supplied to Indian state power companies, increasing fuel costs for consumers and producing greater pollution (McCrum et al., 2023). In the same year, short-seller Hindenburg Research alleged the Adani conglomerate was implicated in stock manipulation and accounting fraud (Hindenburg Research, 2023). In May 2024, the Organised Crime and Corruption Reporting Project (OCCRP) reported it had uncovered extensive evidence in support of the allegations made by *FT* (Mangnale, 2024; McCrum et al., 2023). In November 2024 Gautam Adani and associates were indicted by US authorities for a US$250 million bribery scheme undertaken between 2020 and 2024 to incentivise Indian officials into entering solar energy contracts which were above market value. The alleged bribery scheme was held to violate the 1977 Foreign Corrupt Practices Act (FCPA) because it undermined the integrity of US financial markets in which Adani is engaged (U.S. Attorney's Office, Eastern District of New York, 2024). The indictment also raised new questions about the independence of Indian institutions, namely, the Solar Energy Corporation of India which raised the contract tender, and the Securities and Exchange Board of India (SEBI), whose Chairperson, Madhabi Puri Buch, was accused by Hindenburg of conflicts of interests due to undisclosed stakes in offshore entities used in Adani's malpractices (Korada, 2024; Walia and Barik, 2024).

In the wake of the reports, the Congress and other opposition parties used the indictment and the OCCRP and Hindenburg allegations to launch attacks on Adani and Modi. French oil company TotalEnergies suspended its Adani green energy and gas investments; the US International Development Finance Corp initiated a review of its lending for a Sri Lankan port project leading Adani to withdraw from the agreement; and Sri Lanka sought to renegotiate a power purchase agreement, resulting in Adani abandoning its renewable energy projects there (Kay et al., 2025; Wire, 2024).

The government's attempt to portray India as a safe destination for foreign capital because 'India's democracy is the fundamental base of our country's stability, certainty and sustainable development' has also been challenged internationally (Modi, 2018). In 2021, Freedom House, the Economist Intelligence Unit (EIU) and the V-Dem Institute classified India as a 'partially free democracy', a deteriorating 'flawed democracy', and an 'electoral autocracy' respectively, with Freedom House noting a decline in civil liberties and minority rights since Modi's election in 2014 and an acceleration since 2019. The government was apparently privately 'panicked' by these rankings with Modi particularly concerned about the EIU's Democracy Index because of its potential to damage India's reputation as a safe place for international investment (Dutta, 2023b). Reiterating criticisms of Indian democracy and the Hindenburg allegations, in 2023 financier/philanthropist George Soros criticised Modi, and predicted 'a democratic revival in India' as he faced questions from parliament and foreign investors about his relationship with Adani (Soros, 2023).

### Conspiracy and transnational authoritarianism

The response to criticism of Adani involved a new mode of conspiratorial thinking focussed on global liberal institutions and a common target of the global far-right, George Soros. Soros-centred conspiracies have been a part of authoritarian populist politics in numerous countries including Hungary, Turkey, Brazil, and the US. The signifier of Soros plays a key role in the construction of this emerging transnational populist discourse. He serves as a universal antagonist and part of the equivalential chain that links many different grievances, unifying them into a cohesive demand across different states, attributing crises to a global liberal elite. Such claims have plausibility moreover, because organisations like Freedom House and Soros' Open Society Foundation (OSF) have been long been critiqued for their political and ideological biases and their claims to objectivity. Soros' popular reputation as a short seller who 'broke the Bank of England' also means he can be signified as the source of capitalist crises (Giannone, 2010; Stone, 2010).

Anti-Soros conspiracy theories reproduce antisemitic tropes that he interferes to cause disorder by pulling the strings behind the scenes (Subotic, 2022: 446–447). Reproducing these tropes and tying them to colonialism and anticolonialism, Jaishankar (2023) described Soros as 'old, rich, opinionated and sitting in New York who still thinks his views should determine how the world works'. Such people are dangerous, he said, because 'they actually invest resources in shaping narratives'. This amounted to advocating for 'regime change' and 'influence operations' with the 'gloss of do-goodism', and created a 'fear psychosis' which damaged the societal fabric. This 'worried us' Jaishankar noted, because we 'are a country that went through colonialism, we know the dangers of what happens when there's outside interference'. India, he suggested, had its own version of democracy which was different to 'Euro-Atlantic' notions of democracy.

The Modi regime's Soros conspiracy articulated a new equivalential chain attributing the accusations against Adani to the Congress Party, manipulated by Soros, who was presented as a neo-colonial agent. Protected by the regime, Adani has evaded accountability. The government has refused opposition demands for a parliamentary commission of inquiry. A Supreme Court expert committee (half of which had conflicts of interest) found no evidence of price manipulation; and the Supreme Court deferred to SEBI to investigate the matter (Bhaumik, 2024). A BJP spokesperson, Shezad Poonwalla, claimed that the tweeting of Soros's statement by an adviser to Rahul Gandhi 'proves that the Congress party is not an innocent bystander but a vector of this propaganda and insidious agenda of this self-styled economic war criminal called George Soros' (Quoted in Ghosh, 2023). In response to the Congress's allegation that Modi was a puppet of Adani, the BJP IT Cell released images and a video on social media claiming that Gandhi was a puppet of Soros (Deccan Herald, 2023). A government minister, Smriti Irani accused Soros of wanting to 'break Indian democracy', having 'imperialistic designs', and wanting a government that is 'pliable to his needs' (Quoted in Yadav, 2023). She and the BJP IT Cell later accused Rahul Gandhi of 'hobnobbing with those who are funded by Soros'. This was a reference to Gandhi's meeting with Sunita Viswanathan, the head of a progressive diaspora organisation, Hindus for Human Rights (HFHR), and the presence of a 'global VP of the Open Society Foundation' on Gandhi's Bharat Jodo Yatra (Unite India March) which aimed to rally support for his party (BJP, 2023; Deccan Herald, 2023). The Twitter/X account of HFHR was withheld in India and the organisation was implicated by a website, the Disinfo Lab, which is allegedly funded by Indian intelligence agencies, in an elaborate conspiracy involving Soros and domestic critics of the Modi regime (Shih et al., 2023).

These conspiracies were further expanded to include the US State Department, and the OCCRP, which receives funding from USAID and OSF, and the *FT* in a populist equivalential chain that constructed a fresh universal enemy – the 'Deep State'. In response, the BJP IT Cell claimed the *FT* had close links with Soros, that OCCRP provides narratives to the Congress, and that Rahul Gandhi was connected to the US 'Deep State' which is an 'evil force that has brought nothing but destruction', and 'had a clear objective to destabilise India by targeting Prime Minister Modi' (Quoted in Bhowmick, 2024). These allegations were repeated by BJP Members of Parliament, Sambit Patra and Nishikant Dubey (Bhowmick, 2024; Dayal, 2024). In the immediate aftermath of the indictment, the President William Ruto cancelled Adani's projects in Kenya. This prompted speculation in the prominent pro-government *India Today* news outlet that the decision was the result of pressure from Alex Soros, who now heads OSF, was close to the Biden Administration and attended a state dinner for Ruto (Kumar, 2024b).

By invoking Soros and the Deep State, which are favourite conspiracies of US President Donald Trump, the BJP further builds on the Hindutva movement's long engagement with the global far-right and contributes to the emergence of a reactionary international. In its contemporary form, BJP officials and Hindutva ideologues use obstructive strategies against critics to appeal to Western far-right actors and regimes through shared discourses and networks. Islamophobia and reactionary notions of civilisationalism serve to unite Hindutva groups and ideologues affiliated with the BJP to western far-right figures like Koenraad Elst, Aleksandr Dugin, Richard Spencer and Tommy Robinson (De Gourdon, 2024; Savera, 2022, 2024). The RSS leader Mohan Bhagwat and President of the BJP/RSS-aligned India Foundation, Ram Madhav (2023), recently railed against 'DEI', 'cancel culture', 'cultural Marxism or wokes' for 'capturing institutions like media and academia and plunging education, culture, politics and social environment into chaos'. Madhav (2024)praised speakers at the 2024 National Conservativism Conference, including Viktor Orban, for identifying 'mainstream media, unbridled immigration, political correctness, global NGOs led by people like George Soros, and 'Bolshewokism' as the challenge' and argued India should steer the global conservative movement in the mould of Margaret Thatcher. In his recent book, Madhav (2025) brings together the three conspiracies discussed in this article, blaming Soros, global NGOs, diaspora dissidents and Greta Thunberg for the farmers' protests, citing support from Elon Musk, who accuses Soros of hating humanity. The BJP's youth wing, the Bharatiya Janata Yuva Morcha (BJYM), partnered with the Konrad Adenauer Foundation in a 2024 conference in Delhi on 'conservative politics in an interconnected world', which included discussions on 'wokeism' and involved the Heritage Foundation's Kevin B. Roberts, whose Project 2025 proposal for remaking American governance in autocratic and illiberal directions aligns with many of Trump's presidential orders (ANI, 2024; Durkee, 2025). Among these was an order to suspend the enforcement of the FCPA under which Adani was charged with corruption, on the grounds that it creates an 'uneven playing field' for US businesses against international competitors (Chavez and Palma, 2025). In an example of the transactional deal-making that characterises the reactionary international (Michelsen et al., 2025), Republican politicians have also lobbied the Trump administration not to pursue Adani, due to the lack of harm of its practices to US interests and the damage to US-India relations (PTI, 2025). The rise of the US far-right, thus, diminishes the possibility of global accountability for Indian crony capitalism and, in turn, consolidates India's transnational authoritarianism.

## Conclusion: From *viśvaguru* to *viśvapīḍita*[^1]

As conspiratorial thinking becomes a mainstay of international politics, we need to better understand why conspiracies are promoted by political leaders and what their transnational impact may be. Analysing the Indian government's promotion of transnational conspiracy theories, this article has argued that they can act as tools of populist political articulation, recreating populist cleavages in the face of dissent and criticism, while also legitimising the deepening of autocratic power domestically and extending it transnationally. In these ways, conspiracy theories literally act as an authoritarian 'populist way out' (Wajner, 2022).

While existing works have suggested that Indian foreign policy has been less populist globally than other cases, with the Modi regime emphasising people-centrism and global leadership, this article has shown that particularly after 2019, the regime's *viśvaguru* (teacher of the world) projection has been increasingly been supplemented by one of India as *viśvapīḍita* (oppressed by the world), in which India's rise has been threatened by various transnational conspiracies involving alliances between liberal global and national elites. The timing of these conspiracy theories and deepening authoritarianism, this article has argued, is related to conjunctural conditions – namely, domestic and global resistance to the government's development projects, agricultural reforms, crony capitalism and autocratisation. Populist conspiracy theories related to NGOs, the separatist Khalistan movement and George Soros, resonate with long-standing popular grievances about external interference as well as Hindutva organicist politics. They are used to explain structural crises, for which the regime has few solutions, by generating new populist equivalential chains involving malign internal and external actors.

India's use of populist conspiracy theories facilitates transnational authoritarian practices including the transnational repression of diaspora dissidents through threats, digital censorship, detention and alleged assassination; the repression of NGOs; election interference and dis/misinformation in other states. These conspiracy theories and authoritarian practices have involved various transnational actors, such as social media companies that comply with censorship orders; international institutions like Interpol, the FATF and the UN, which provide avenues for repression; and global far-right political networks which supply legitimising tropes. India has furthered the creation of illiberal, autocratic global norms related to cyber governance and NGOs and by engaging with far-right discourses and networks, it contributes to the emergence of a reactionary international. The tensions within, and the contested nature of these interconnected political currents, however, means that the longevity of these links and practices and their influence on global order remains unpredictable.
